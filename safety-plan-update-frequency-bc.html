How Often Should You Update Your Safety Plan in BC?

A safety plan is not a one-time document. Learn when and how often to update your program to stay compliant with WorkSafeBC requirements.

A safety plan is not a document you write once and forget. It is a living system that must evolve with your workplace. Stale documentation is one of the most common findings in WorkSafeBC inspections and client audits.

This article explains when and how often to update your safety plan to stay compliant and effective.

The Legal Baseline

WorkSafeBC requires employers to review their health and safety program at least annually. This is the minimum. In practice, you should update your program whenever something changes that affects worker safety.

An annual review is not just a calendar event. It is an opportunity to assess whether your program still matches your workplace. If it does not, you must update it.

Trigger Events That Require Immediate Updates

Certain events should trigger an immediate review and update of your safety program, regardless of when the annual review is scheduled.

New Equipment or Processes

When you introduce new equipment, new processes, or new materials, your hazard assessments and procedures must be updated to reflect them. A program that does not address your current equipment is inadequate.

Changes in Workforce

Significant changes in your workforce affect your safety program. New workers need orientation. New supervisors need training. Changes in crew size may affect your Joint Health and Safety Committee requirements.

Regulatory Changes

When WorkSafeBC updates the OHS Regulation, you must assess whether the changes affect your program. Some regulatory changes require immediate action. Others may be phased in. Stay informed.

Incidents

After any incident, your investigation should identify whether your program contributed to the incident. If it did, update the program. Even if it did not, review whether similar incidents could occur elsewhere.

Client or Contractor Requirements

If a client or contractor imposes new safety requirements, your program must address them. This is common in construction where general contractors have specific pre-qualification standards.

Site Changes

If you move to a new location, renovate your existing space, or add a new work area, your program must be updated to reflect the new conditions.

The Annual Review Process

An annual review should be systematic. It should cover all elements of your program and involve the people who use it. Here is a process to follow.

Step 1: Gather Information

Collect data from the past year. This includes inspection records, incident reports, near miss reports, training records, and worker feedback. This data tells you what is working and what is not.

Step 2: Review Each Element

Go through your program element by element. Is your policy current? Are your hazard assessments accurate? Do your procedures match actual practice? Are your training records complete?

Step 3: Consult Workers

Ask workers what is working and what is not. They are the ones doing the work. They know where the gaps are. Your Joint Health and Safety Committee should be involved in this review.

Step 4: Identify Gaps

Based on your review, identify gaps between your program and your actual workplace. These are the areas that need updating.

Step 5: Make Updates

Update the program to address the gaps. This may involve rewriting procedures, updating hazard assessments, or adding new elements.

Step 6: Communicate Changes

Tell workers what has changed. Train them on updated procedures. Update posted documents.

Step 7: Document the Review

Document that the review occurred, who was involved, what was found, and what was changed. This documentation proves your program is current.

Signs Your Program Is Stale

These are the signs that your program needs updating:

  • Procedures that do not match how work is actually done.
  • Equipment listed in your hazard assessments that you no longer use.
  • Training records that are more than a year old for current workers.
  • Contact information for emergency contacts that is out of date.
  • References to regulations that have changed.
  • Workers who cannot explain the procedures they are supposed to follow.

Any of these signs indicates your program has fallen behind your workplace.

The Cost of Stale Documentation

Stale documentation creates several problems. WorkSafeBC inspectors will issue orders for outdated programs. Client auditors will reject your pre-qualification application. Your workers will not follow procedures that do not match reality. And you will miss the opportunity to learn from incidents and near misses.

An outdated program is worse than no program in some ways. It gives the illusion of compliance while providing no real protection.

Making Updates a Habit

The best way to keep your program current is to make updates a habit, not an event. Assign responsibility for program maintenance to a specific person. Schedule regular reviews. Encourage workers to report when procedures do not match practice.

Consider using a compliance calendar to track when reviews are due. This helps ensure nothing is missed.

The Baseline Schedule

As a minimum, review your safety plan annually. Mark it in the calendar and treat it like a regulatory deadline, because in practice it is one. The annual review is a systematic pass through every section: hazards, procedures, training, emergency contacts, first aid, committee structure, and anything else the plan covers. Outdated content is corrected or removed, and the revision is documented.

Between annual reviews, certain events should trigger an immediate review of the affected sections:

  • Any incident or near miss with injury potential.
  • New equipment, new materials, or new work processes.
  • A change of site, expansion, or new line of business.
  • New regulatory requirements or revised WorkSafeBC guidance.
  • An inspection, order, or audit finding.
  • Significant workforce growth that changes committee or first aid thresholds.

How to Run the Review Efficiently

Full rewrites are expensive and usually unnecessary. Structure the review as a gap check. Compare each section against current reality. Ask supervisors and workers what changed this year. Their answers identify the sections needing attention far faster than reading the binder alone. Update only what changed, record the revision date, and communicate material changes to the workforce. A plan that was revised but never communicated creates a new gap while closing an old one.

Version Control

Maintain version dates on every controlled document inside the plan. Remove superseded pages from circulation, since mixed versions cause workers to follow outdated procedures. Assign one person ownership of the master copy. When audits or inspections arrive, being able to show a documented revision history demonstrates that the program is managed rather than neglected.

Who Should Own the Update Process

Updates fail when everyone assumes someone else handles them. Assign clear ownership: one person accountable for the master plan, supervisors responsible for flagging changes in their areas, and the joint committee reviewing proposed revisions. When a procedure changes because equipment changed, the loop closes fastest if the supervisor who knows about the change has a direct path to the person who owns the document. Build that path explicitly.

Communicating Changes to the Workforce

A revised plan that workers have not heard about does not protect them or you. Material changes need communication: toolbox talks for procedure changes, signage for physical changes, and updated orientation content for new requirements. Keep a record of the communication alongside the revision. In an inspection, being able to show when the plan changed and how workers were informed demonstrates a managed program rather than a shelf document.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an annual review mandatory? The regulation requires programs to be reviewed regularly, and annual is the accepted baseline for most operations. Certain events trigger reviews sooner, independent of the calendar.

What if nothing changed this year? Document the review anyway. Confirming that hazards, procedures, and contacts remain current is itself a finding, and the record shows the program is actively managed.

Who signs off on revisions? Whoever owns the master plan, with visibility from supervisors and the joint committee where material changes affect workers.

Do workers need retraining after every update? Only when content changes in ways that affect their work. Communicate material changes through toolbox talks or orientation updates, and keep records of the communication.

The Bottom Line

Update frequency is really a question of program ownership. Plans owned actively change when the operation changes and pass reviews effortlessly. Plans owned nominally drift until an inspection or incident exposes the distance. Choose active ownership: annual reviews on the calendar, event-triggered reviews in the procedure, and version control that makes the current plan obvious. The discipline is modest, and the payoff lasts for years.

A simple calendar entry does most of the work: schedule the annual review for the same month each year, assign the owner in advance, and block the time. When the review becomes a routine appointment rather than an occasional reaction, keeping the plan current stops competing with daily operations for attention. The employers with the strongest programs are rarely the ones with the most resources. They are the ones with the most reliable routines.
