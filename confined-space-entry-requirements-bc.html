Confined Space Entry Requirements in British Columbia

Confined space entry is one of the deadliest workplace hazards. Learn what BC employers must do to comply with WorkSafeBC requirements and protect wor

Confined space entry kills more workers than almost any other workplace hazard. The danger is not just the confined space itself. It is the atmosphere inside it. Oxygen deficiency, toxic gases, and flammable vapors can incapacitate a worker in seconds. And would-be rescuers account for a shocking percentage of confined space fatalities.

British Columbia has specific requirements for confined space entry under Part 9 of the OHS Regulation. This article explains what those requirements are and how to comply.

What Is a Confined Space

A confined space is an enclosed or partially enclosed area that is not designed for continuous human occupancy and has limited means of entry and exit. Common examples include tanks, vessels, sewers, manholes, pipelines, silos, and storage bins.

What makes confined spaces dangerous is not the physical dimensions. It is what can accumulate inside them. Oxygen can be displaced by other gases or consumed by rust, decomposition, or bacterial action. Toxic gases like hydrogen sulfide can build up from decomposing organic matter. Flammable vapors can reach explosive concentrations.

The Legal Requirements

Part 9 of the OHS Regulation sets out specific requirements for confined space entry. Employers must:

Identify Confined Spaces

You must identify all confined spaces in your workplace and assess their hazards. This assessment determines whether the space is a permit-required confined space or not.

Develop an Entry Program

You must have a written confined space entry program if your workers enter confined spaces. This program must include procedures for hazard assessment, atmospheric testing, ventilation, communication, rescue, and entry permits.

Test the Atmosphere

The atmosphere inside a confined space must be tested before entry and continuously during entry. Testing must check oxygen levels, toxic gases, and flammable vapors. Testing must be done by a qualified person using calibrated equipment.

Use an Entry Permit

Entry into a permit-required confined space must be authorized by a written permit. The permit confirms that all precautions have been taken, the atmosphere is safe, rescue arrangements are in place, and the entrant is qualified.

Provide Rescue Arrangements

You must have rescue arrangements in place before entry. This means either a rescue team stationed outside the space or equipment and procedures for self-rescue or external rescue. The rescue plan must be specific to the space and the hazards.

Train Workers

All workers involved in confined space entry must be trained on the hazards, the procedures, and their roles. This includes entrants, attendants, and supervisors.

Common Confined Space Hazards

Oxygen Deficiency

Normal air contains about 21 percent oxygen. Below 19.5 percent, the atmosphere is oxygen-deficient. At 16 percent, judgment and coordination are impaired. Below 10 percent, loss of consciousness is rapid and death can follow.

Toxic Gases

Hydrogen sulfide, carbon monoxide, and methane are common in confined spaces. Hydrogen sulfide is particularly dangerous because it deadens the sense of smell at high concentrations, so workers cannot detect it by odor.

Engulfment

Loose materials like grain, sawdust, or sand can engulf a worker in seconds. Engulfment is a particular hazard in silos and bins.

Moving Machinery

Agitators, augers, and other moving parts inside confined spaces must be locked out before entry. Failure to lock out is a common cause of confined space fatalities.

The Entry Permit Process

A confined space entry permit is a checklist that confirms all safety precautions are in place before entry. The permit typically includes:

  • The location and description of the space.
  • The hazards identified.
  • The atmospheric test results.
  • The precautions taken (ventilation, lockout, etc.).
  • The names of the entrants and attendants.
  • The rescue arrangements.
  • The authorized signature.
  • The entry and exit times.

The permit must be completed before entry and kept on file.

Common Mistakes

The most common confined space mistakes are:

  • Entering without testing the atmosphere first.
  • Relying on smell to detect toxic gases.
  • Skipping the permit because the job will only take a few minutes.
  • Not having rescue arrangements in place.
  • Failing to lock out moving machinery inside the space.
  • Not training workers on their roles.

Any one of these mistakes can be fatal.

Rescue: The Critical Element

The majority of confined space fatalities involve would-be rescuers. When a worker collapses inside a confined space, the natural instinct is to rush in and help. Without proper training and equipment, the rescuer becomes the second victim.

Never enter a confined space to attempt a rescue without proper training, equipment, and atmospheric testing. Call emergency services. Use retrieval equipment if available. Your rescue plan must be practiced before it is needed.

What Counts as a Confined Space

Many employers are surprised by what qualifies. A confined space is not just a tank or a vault. It includes any area that is enclosed or partially enclosed, not designed for continuous occupancy, and with limited entry and exit. Manholes, sumps, pits, silos, sewers, vessels, and some crawl spaces all qualify. If workers enter these areas, even briefly, the regulatory requirements attach.

Some confined spaces also require classification as requiring a permit, typically because of atmospheric hazards or internal configuration risks. The permit system adds planning requirements: written procedures, assigned roles, atmospheric testing, standby attendance, rescue arrangements, and documentation of each entry.

The Entry Roles and Why They Matter

A compliant confined space entry involves defined roles. The entrant performs the work. The attendant monitors from outside, maintains communication, and initiates rescue if something goes wrong. The entry supervisor authorizes the entry and confirms all precautions are in place. Rescue personnel, whether internal or external, must be arranged before entry, not after an incident.

Most confined space fatalities involve would-be rescuers. When a worker collapses inside, the instinct to rush in is powerful and lethal. A standby attendant with proper training and equipment is the control that breaks this pattern. This is why cutting corners on attendant coverage is treated so seriously.

Building Your Program Step by Step

If confined space entry applies to your operation, build the program in order:

  • Inventory every confined space at your worksites and classify each one.
  • Write entry procedures per space or space type, including isolation and lockout requirements.
  • Procure and calibrate atmospheric testing equipment, and train workers to use it.
  • Establish rescue arrangements and verify they can actually reach your spaces within the required time.
  • Train and drill every worker with an assigned role.
  • Document entries with permits and retain records.

Each step builds on the previous one, and skipping early steps makes later ones cosmetic.

Atmospheric Testing: The Detail That Decides

Most confined space fatalities involve oxygen deficiency or toxic gases, and most of those were detectable before entry. Atmospheric testing is therefore the centre of the program. Testing must occur before entry and continuously during work where conditions can change. The order of testing matters: oxygen first, then flammable gases, then toxic contaminants. Instruments must be calibrated, bump-tested, and used by trained workers who understand what the readings mean.

Ventilation is the companion control. Forced ventilation can reduce concentrations to safe levels, but it must be maintained for the duration of the work and verified with monitoring. Assuming fresh air once means fresh air always is a dangerous error, because conditions inside a confined space can change with the work itself.

Training and Drills

Written procedures without trained people are decoration. Every worker with an entry role needs training matched to that role, and the team needs to drill together. Drills expose problems: radios that do not work in steel vessels, rescue equipment stored too far from the entry point, attendants unsure of their authority to stop entry. Run a drill before the first entry of each season and after any significant roster change. The half-day cost is trivial against what a failed real rescue would mean.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a manhole count as a confined space? Yes. Manholes, sumps, tanks, vessels, silos, and pits all meet the definition. If the space is enclosed, not designed for continuous occupancy, and has limited entry and exit, the requirements attach when workers enter.

Can one person enter alone if the job is quick? No. Entry requires assigned roles, standby attendance, and the supporting procedures regardless of duration. Most confined space fatalities involve workers who entered briefly to finish a small task.

Who can perform the rescue? Rescue must be pre-arranged before entry, either with trained internal responders and equipment or through an external rescue service confirmed to reach your location within the required time. Hope is not a rescue plan.

How often must atmospheric testers be calibrated? Follow the manufacturer's schedule, which typically requires regular calibration and bump testing before each use. An uncalibrated tester gives false confidence, which is worse than no tester at all.

The Bottom Line

Confined space work rewards preparation and punishes improvisation. Every fatality in this category traces back to a skipped step: no testing, no attendant, no rescue plan, or a rescuer who became the second victim. The requirements exist because the pattern is that consistent. If your operation involves entry, build the program properly, drill it regularly, and treat every shortcut as the risk it is.
