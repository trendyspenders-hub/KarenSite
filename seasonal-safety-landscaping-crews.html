Seasonal Safety: What Landscaping Crews Must Document Before Spring

Landscaping crews face unique seasonal hazards. Learn what safety documentation you need before spring rush and how to prepare your crew.

Spring is the busiest season for BC landscaping companies. Crews are hiring, equipment is coming out of storage, and clients are calling. In the rush to get started, safety documentation often gets overlooked. That is a mistake.

This article covers what landscaping crews must document before the spring rush and how to prepare your crew for a safe season.

Why Spring Is High-Risk

Several factors make spring the highest-risk season for landscaping crews:

  • New workers who have never done the job before.
  • Equipment that has been sitting unused for months.
  • Weather conditions that change rapidly.
  • Tight schedules that pressure crews to skip steps.
  • Tasks that only happen once a year, like tree work and pesticide application.

Proper documentation before the season starts is your best defense against the risks these factors create.

What You Must Document

New Worker Orientation

Every new worker must be oriented before they start work. This is a legal requirement. Your orientation must cover workplace hazards, safe work procedures, emergency procedures, and worker rights. It must be documented with the worker's signature.

For landscaping, orientation should include specific hazards like equipment operation, traffic exposure, heat stress, and pesticide handling if applicable.

Equipment Inspections

All equipment must be inspected before use. This includes mowers, trimmers, chainsaws, chippers, and vehicles. Inspections must be documented. Equipment that fails inspection must be repaired or removed from service.

Create an inspection checklist for each piece of equipment. Have operators complete it before the first use of the season and regularly thereafter.

Safe Work Procedures

Every high-risk task needs a written procedure. For landscaping, this includes:

  • Mower and trimmer operation.
  • Chainsaw use and tree work.
  • Chemical and pesticide handling.
  • Traffic control for roadside work.
  • Working in extreme heat.

Review these procedures with your crew before the season starts. Update them if anything has changed.

Training Records

Document all training. This includes orientation, equipment-specific training, and any certifications like pesticide applicator licenses. Keep these records organized and accessible.

First Aid

Ensure your first aid kits are stocked and your first aid attendants have current certificates. Check expiry dates before the season starts.

Seasonal Hazards to Address

Heat Stress

BC summers are getting hotter. Heat stress is a serious hazard for outdoor workers. Your program must address heat stress prevention, including hydration schedules, rest breaks, and recognition of heat illness symptoms.

Traffic Exposure

Roadside work puts crews at risk from passing vehicles. You must have traffic control procedures for any work near a roadway. This includes proper signage, flagging procedures, and high-visibility clothing.

Equipment Hazards

Landscaping equipment is dangerous. Mowers throw debris. Chainsaws cause serious cuts. Chippers can grab clothing. Every operator must be trained on the specific equipment they use.

Chemical Exposure

Pesticides, herbicides, and fertilizers all carry risks. Workers must be trained on safe handling, storage, and disposal. SDS sheets must be available for all chemicals on site.

Weather

Spring weather in BC is unpredictable. Crews must be prepared for rain, wind, and rapid temperature changes. Have procedures for suspending work in dangerous conditions.

How to Prepare Before the Rush

Start early. Do not wait until April to get your documentation in order. Here is a timeline.

February: Program Review

Review your safety program. Update procedures that have changed. Check expiry dates on training records and certifications. Order any missing supplies.

March: Equipment and Training

Inspect all equipment. Conduct refresher training for returning workers. Orient new hires. Update your hazard assessments for the coming season.

April: Final Checks

Confirm all documentation is complete. Verify first aid supplies and certifications. Brief your crew on the season's plans and safety expectations.

The Cost of Being Unprepared

An unprepared season is an expensive season. Incidents cost time and money. WorkSafeBC orders disrupt your schedule. And the human cost of a preventable injury is the highest price of all.

A few days of preparation in February and March can save you months of headaches later.

The Seasonal Safety Problem

Landscaping businesses run on a compressed calendar. The season starts fast, crews scale up quickly with a mix of returning and brand new workers, and everyone is busy from the first warm week. Safety systems that were adequate for a small off-season crew suddenly have to cover three times the people, new equipment, and jobs spread across many sites. This is exactly when incidents cluster.

WorkSafeBC data consistently shows elevated injury rates in landscaping and horticulture compared to many other sectors. The hazards are physical and constant: rotating blades, thrown objects, vibration, heat, repetitive lifting, noise, traffic, and pesticides. None of these are exotic. All of them are manageable with a program that was actually built for this work.

A Pre-Season Safety Checklist

Run through this list before your crews hit full speed:

  • Service and inspect all equipment, including guards, emergency stops, and operator presence controls.
  • Confirm operator training and supervision arrangements for every machine, especially for new hires.
  • Refresh hazard assessments for each site type you serve, noting traffic, slopes, underground services, and public interface.
  • Restock personal protective equipment: eye and hearing protection, gloves, high-visibility clothing, and respirators where required.
  • Review heat stress procedures and hydration plans for summer months.
  • Confirm first aid kits, eye-wash arrangements, and emergency procedures for each crew.
  • Schedule orientations for every worker, including experienced returning staff, because procedures change.

Print the list, assign owners, and check items off. The exercise takes a day or two and pays for itself many times over.

Building Safety Into a Mobile Crew

Landscaping crews move between sites constantly, which means the workplace changes daily while the hazards follow familiar patterns. The solution is a program that travels: a short tailgate meeting routine covering the day's site hazards, a compact hazard assessment format supervisors can complete on-site, and documentation light enough to keep current. If your safety paperwork requires an hour of office time per crew per day, it will be abandoned by June. Design for the reality of the work and the program will still be running in September.

Crew-Level Practices That Hold Up

Beyond the pre-season checklist, certain daily practices separate safe landscaping operations from lucky ones. Start each day with a short crew talk covering the sites scheduled, their specific hazards, and any equipment issues. Assign a worker to lead the talk on rotation so the skill spreads. End serious incidents and near misses with a five-minute debrief before the next shift. These small routines cost almost nothing and catch problems while they are still small.

Equipment discipline matters equally. Walk-around inspections before operation, reported defects taken out of service immediately, and blades and guards never modified. The pace of seasonal work pressures crews to skip these steps, which is precisely why the discipline must be non-negotiable from the owner down.

Working Near the Public

Residential and commercial landscaping puts crews beside the public: homeowners, pedestrians, passing vehicles. This adds hazards your workers do not control. Establish buffer zones around mowing and trimming operations, use signage and cones consistently, and train crews to stop work when the public enters the work zone. Many serious incidents in this sector involve members of the public, and the liability consequences exceed any job's profit. Plan for the public interface the same way you plan for the hazards you own.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common landscaping injury? Patterns typically include cuts and amputations from blades, musculoskeletal injuries from lifting and vibration, and eye injuries from thrown debris. All three respond to the same controls: equipment discipline, proper PPE, and honest hazard assessment per site.

Do I need a written safety plan for a seasonal business? If you employ workers in British Columbia, the requirement to have a health and safety program applies regardless of season length. Seasonal operations need programs designed for their reality: mobile, compact, and crew-friendly.

How do I orient crews who only work a few months? The same standard applies as for year-round workers: orientation before work begins, documented and specific to the hazards they will face. Seasonal status does not reduce the requirement or the risk.

What PPE is essential for landscaping? At minimum: eye protection, hearing protection, gloves, high-visibility clothing, and sturdy footwear. Task-specific additions include face protection for string trimming and respirators where dust or pesticide exposure applies.

The Bottom Line

Seasonal operations cannot afford a slow safety ramp. The crews are new, the schedule is full, and the hazards do not wait for paperwork. Build the program in the off-season, run the checklist before the first job, and keep daily routines light enough to survive the busiest week. Employers who manage this treat safety as part of production planning, and it shows in their incident record, their insurance costs, and their ability to keep experienced crews returning season after season.
