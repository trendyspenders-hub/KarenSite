New Worker Orientation Requirements in British Columbia

BC employers must orient new workers before they start work. Learn what the orientation must cover, how to document it, and what happens if you skip i

Every new worker in British Columbia must receive a workplace orientation before starting work. This is not a courtesy. It is a legal requirement under the Workers Compensation Act and the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation.

Despite this, new worker orientation is one of the most commonly missed elements in BC workplaces. Employers are busy. Workers are eager to start. The orientation gets skipped or shortened. Then an incident happens and the missing documentation becomes a serious problem.

What the Law Requires

Section 34 of the Workers Compensation Act and Part 3 of the OHS Regulation require employers to provide new workers with orientation to the workplace. This orientation must cover the hazards they will face, the procedures they must follow, and their rights and responsibilities.

The regulation is specific about timing. Orientation must happen before the worker starts performing their duties. Not after. Not on their second day. Before they begin work.

Who Counts as a New Worker

A new worker is not just someone brand new to your company. The definition includes:

  • Workers new to your workplace, even if they have industry experience.
  • Workers returning to a job after an absence of more than six months.
  • Workers moving to a new job within your company that has different hazards.
  • Young workers (under 25) who may face additional risks due to inexperience.

Each of these situations requires orientation specific to the new role or workplace.

What the Orientation Must Cover

A compliant new worker orientation covers these areas.

Workplace Hazards

The worker must be informed of the specific hazards they will encounter. This includes physical hazards like machinery, heights, and chemicals. It also includes workplace-specific hazards like traffic patterns, confined spaces, or extreme temperatures.

Safe Work Procedures

The worker must be trained on the procedures relevant to their job. This means more than handing them a binder. It means walking them through the procedures, demonstrating them where possible, and confirming understanding.

Emergency Procedures

The worker must know what to do in an emergency. Where are the exits? Where is the muster point? Who is the first aid attendant? How do they report an emergency?

Hazard Reporting

The worker must know how to report a hazard or concern. They must understand their right to refuse unsafe work without fear of retaliation. This is a critical legal protection.

Personal Protective Equipment

The worker must be shown the PPE required for their job, how to use it, and how to maintain it. PPE must be provided at no cost to the worker.

Worker Rights and Responsibilities

The worker must understand their three fundamental rights: the right to know about hazards, the right to participate in safety decisions, and the right to refuse unsafe work. They must also understand their responsibility to follow procedures and report hazards.

How to Document Orientation

Documentation is critical. If you cannot prove the orientation happened, you cannot demonstrate compliance. At minimum, your orientation records should include:

  • The worker's name and signature.
  • The date of orientation.
  • The topics covered.
  • The name of the person conducting the orientation.
  • Confirmation that the worker understands their rights and responsibilities.

Keep these records for at least the duration of employment plus a reasonable period afterward. WorkSafeBC may ask for them years later during an investigation.

Young Workers: Special Considerations

Workers under 25 face a higher risk of workplace injury. This is not because they are careless. It is because they lack experience and may not recognize hazards that experienced workers take for granted.

For young workers, orientation must be more thorough. It must account for their limited workplace experience. Supervision should be increased. Procedures should be demonstrated, not just described. And the worker must feel comfortable asking questions without fear of appearing incompetent.

Common Mistakes

The most common orientation mistakes are:

  • Skipping orientation entirely for experienced workers new to the company.
  • Relying on a binder or video without verbal explanation and demonstration.
  • Failing to document the orientation.
  • Not covering workplace-specific hazards that differ from industry norms.
  • Treating orientation as a one-time event without follow-up.

Building an Orientation Program

A good orientation program is structured and repeatable. It should include a checklist of topics to cover, a standard script or outline, a documentation form, and a follow-up process. New workers should receive a copy of the relevant procedures and emergency contacts.

Consider assigning a mentor or buddy to new workers for their first few weeks. This gives them someone to ask questions of and helps them integrate into your safety culture.

What Happens If You Skip Orientation

If a new worker is injured and you cannot prove they were oriented, the consequences are serious. WorkSafeBC can issue orders and penalties. Your liability increases. And the worker's injury may have been preventable with proper orientation.

Beyond the legal consequences, skipping orientation sends a message to your workers that safety is not a priority. That message has long-term effects on your safety culture.

What Orientation Must Cover

A useful orientation walks the worker through the hazards they will actually face, not hazards in general. For a construction site, that means fall hazards, overhead work, mobile equipment, and site-specific rules. For a warehouse, it means racking, forklifts, manual handling, and dock areas. Generic videos about workplace safety in general do not satisfy the requirement on their own, because they cannot address your specific workplace.

At minimum, the orientation should cover:

  • The hazards of the job and the work area, identified in your current hazard assessment.
  • The safe work procedures that apply to the tasks the worker will perform.
  • How to report hazards, incidents, and injuries, and who to report them to.
  • Emergency procedures, including evacuation routes, muster points, and first aid arrangements.
  • Required personal protective equipment and where to obtain it.
  • Training requirements, supervision arrangements, and probationary expectations where applicable.

Document everything. A signature sheet is a start, but better records include what was covered, who delivered it, the date, and any questions the worker asked.

The Supervision Question

Orientation and supervision are linked. A worker who has not yet demonstrated competence in a task needs direct supervision while performing it. Many incidents involving new workers happen in the first weeks on the job, when the worker knows enough to start work but not enough to recognize when something is wrong. Planning supervision deliberately during this window is one of the most effective risk controls available to an employer.

Common Orientation Mistakes

These patterns show up repeatedly in incident investigations:

  • Orientation completed on paper but never actually delivered.
  • A one-size-fits-all orientation used across very different roles and sites.
  • No orientation at all for experienced hires, on the assumption that experience transfers.
  • Orientation delivered by someone who was not themselves trained on the content.
  • No follow-up to confirm the worker understood the material.

Each of these is easy to fix once it is named. The harder part is building a routine so orientation happens every time, for every worker, without exception.

Young Workers and the Enhanced Duties

British Columbia places extra emphasis on young and new workers because their injury rates are consistently higher. Experience from other workplaces does not transfer automatically to yours, and overconfidence is a real risk factor. Supervisors should assign tasks progressively, confirm understanding before moving to higher-risk work, and check in more frequently during the first weeks. A young worker who feels comfortable asking questions is safer than one who nods to avoid looking inexperienced.

Orientation Records That Protect You

If an incident occurs and the investigation asks whether the worker was oriented, your records are the answer. Keep orientation records that include the date, content covered, who delivered it, and the worker's acknowledgement. Add a note about any follow-up coaching. These records protect the worker by confirming they received the information, and they protect the employer by demonstrating due diligence. Paper orientation without delivery is a liability dressed up as protection; delivery without records is protection that cannot be proven. Do both.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does orientation apply to experienced hires? Yes. Every new worker must be oriented to your specific workplace, regardless of prior experience. Experience in the trade does not include knowledge of your site, your equipment, your procedures, or your reporting channels.

How long should orientation take? Long enough to cover the actual hazards and procedures, which varies by role and risk. For low-risk environments, an hour may suffice. For higher-risk work, orientation plus supervised practice over days or weeks is more realistic.

What records should I keep? Keep the date, content covered, who delivered it, and the worker's acknowledgement. Add follow-up notes where coaching occurred. These records demonstrate due diligence if an incident is later investigated.

Who should deliver orientation? Someone competent in the content, which usually means a trained supervisor or safety lead. Delivery by someone who does not understand the material teaches workers that safety information is not serious.
