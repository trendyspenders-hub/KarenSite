Incident Investigation Requirements Under WorkSafeBC

BC employers must investigate workplace incidents to find root causes. Learn what must be investigated, how to investigate, and how to document findin

When a workplace incident occurs in British Columbia, the employer has legal obligations. Serious incidents must be reported to WorkSafeBC immediately. All incidents must be investigated. The purpose of investigation is not to assign blame. It is to find the root cause and prevent recurrence.

This article explains what must be investigated, how to investigate, and how to document your findings.

What Must Be Reported and Investigated

Under Part 5 of the OHS Regulation, employers must report certain incidents to WorkSafeBC immediately. These include:

  • Serious injuries requiring medical attention beyond first aid.
  • Fatalities.
  • Incidents involving hazardous substances.
  • Structural collapses.
  • Explosions or fires.
  • Incidents that could have caused serious injury (near misses).

All incidents, including minor injuries and near misses, must be investigated by the employer. The depth of investigation should be proportional to the severity and potential severity of the incident.

The Purpose of Investigation

The purpose of incident investigation is to identify root causes and implement corrective actions. It is not to assign blame or punish workers.

Blame-focused investigations miss the point. If a worker makes an error, the question is not "why did they make that error?" The question is "what in our system allowed that error to happen?" Was the procedure unclear? Was training insufficient? Was equipment poorly maintained? Was the workload unreasonable?

Root cause analysis looks beyond the immediate cause to the underlying system failures. This is where real prevention happens.

How to Investigate

Secure the Scene

After an incident, secure the scene to prevent further injury and preserve evidence. Do not move equipment or materials unless necessary to prevent further harm.

Provide Medical Care

Ensure injured workers receive medical care. This is the first priority. Reporting and investigation come after.

Report to WorkSafeBC

If the incident is serious, report it to WorkSafeBC immediately. Do not wait for a full investigation. Initial reports can be updated as more information becomes available.

Gather Information

Collect facts about what happened. Interview witnesses separately. Take photographs. Review relevant procedures and training records. Examine equipment involved.

Identify Root Causes

Analyze the information to identify root causes. Ask "why" repeatedly. Why did the incident happen? Because the procedure was not followed. Why was the procedure not followed? Because the worker was not trained on it. Why was the worker not trained? Because there is no process to ensure training is completed. This is a root cause.

Implement Corrective Actions

Develop corrective actions that address the root causes. Assign responsibility and deadlines. Follow up to confirm completion.

Documentation Requirements

Incident investigations must be documented. The documentation should include:

  • The date, time, and location of the incident.
  • The names of workers involved and witnesses.
  • A description of what happened.
  • The injuries or damage that resulted.
  • The root causes identified.
  • The corrective actions implemented.
  • The names of investigators.

Keep these records for at least the duration required by regulation. WorkSafeBC may request them years later.

Common Investigation Mistakes

These are the most common mistakes in incident investigation:

Blaming the Worker

Concluding that the incident was caused by "worker error" without asking why the error occurred. This misses the opportunity to fix the system.

Inadequate Investigation

Conducting a superficial investigation that identifies only the immediate cause. This leaves root causes uncorrected.

No Follow-Through

Identifying corrective actions but not implementing them. This is worse than not investigating at all because it creates a paper trail of known unaddressed hazards.

Poor Documentation

Failing to document the investigation properly. This creates liability and prevents learning from the incident.

Delaying the Investigation

Waiting too long to investigate. Evidence disappears. Witnesses forget. The opportunity to learn is lost.

Near Misses: The Free Lessons

A near miss is an incident that could have caused serious injury but did not. Near misses are free lessons. They reveal the same hazards as serious incidents without the human cost.

Encourage workers to report near misses without fear of blame. Investigate near misses with the same rigor as serious incidents. The information you gain can prevent the next serious incident.

The Role of the JHSC

Your Joint Health and Safety Committee must participate in incident investigations. This includes reviewing investigation reports and recommending corrective actions. The JHSC brings worker perspective to the investigation and helps ensure that recommendations are implemented.

Building an Investigation Process

A good incident investigation process is structured and repeatable. It includes:

  • Clear criteria for what must be investigated.
  • Trained investigators.
  • Standard investigation forms and templates.
  • A process for tracking corrective actions to completion.
  • A process for sharing lessons learned.

What Must Be Reported and When

Reporting obligations scale with severity. Serious injuries and fatalities must be reported to WorkSafeBC immediately, with specific information provided in a defined window. Incidents with a high potential for serious harm, sometimes called near misses with serious potential, also carry reporting duties. Less serious injuries follow the claims and reporting processes through your WorkSafeBC account.

Many employers handle the immediate reporting correctly but stumble on the investigation requirement. The law expects employers to conduct an investigation, with worker and committee participation, whenever a serious incident occurs. The investigation must identify root causes and produce corrective actions. Skipping straight to a claims form without investigating leaves the hazard in place.

From Root Cause to Corrective Action

The purpose of an investigation is prevention, not blame. A blame-focused investigation produces defensive answers and hidden information. A systems-focused investigation asks why the conditions existed: Was the procedure adequate? Was training current? Was supervision appropriate? Was equipment maintained? Was the pace of work realistic?

Corrective actions must match the root causes. Retraining a worker after a guarding incident, when the guard design was the real problem, fixes nothing. Each corrective action should have an owner and a deadline, and the follow-up to confirm completion is part of the investigation, not an optional extra.

Documentation That Holds Up

Good investigation records include the facts gathered, the analysis performed, the conclusions reached, and the actions committed. Preserve the scene where required before anything is moved. Photograph conditions, collect statements early while memory is fresh, and involve the worker representative. Records should be retained according to regulatory requirements and treated as learning tools during future orientations and committee reviews.

Near Misses: The Free Lessons

Near misses are incidents where nothing was hurt but something could have been. They are the cheapest learning an operation will ever receive, and they mostly go unreported because workers fear blame or paperwork. Employers who build a genuine no-blame reporting culture, who investigate near misses with the same rigor as injuries at a level proportionate to potential, gain years of warning that other employers pay for in injuries.

Make reporting easy and visibly consequence-free. Review reported near misses at committee meetings, act on them, and tell the reporter what changed. Workers watch whether reporting leads to improvement. If it does, reporting grows. If it leads to discipline, the information stream stops and the next event arrives without warning.

When to Get Outside Help

Some investigations benefit from independent expertise: serious injuries, events with legal exposure, or situations where internal objectivity is in doubt. External investigators bring method and distance, and their reports carry weight with regulators, insurers, and counsel. Even in routine investigations, a periodic external review of your investigation quality sharpens the internal process. The skill of finding root cause is trainable, and it improves with deliberate practice and honest critique.

Frequently Asked Questions

What counts as a serious injury for reporting? The regulation defines serious injuries, and the definitions matter. When in doubt, report. Reporting late is treated more seriously than reporting conservatively.

Who participates in the investigation? The employer conducts it, with participation from worker representatives and the joint committee where one exists. The worker involved participates, because their account of events is essential evidence.

How fast must an investigation happen? Promptly, while evidence and memory are fresh. For reportable incidents, specific timelines apply to both the initial report and the investigation itself.

Can investigation records be used against us? Investigation records serve prevention first. Be honest and thorough in them. Records that show a genuine system approach to learning are far more defensible than records that appear to minimize or deflect.

The Bottom Line

Investigations convert incidents into information. Without them, the same conditions produce the same injuries on a schedule nobody controls. With them, each event sharpens the program and the workforce's understanding of it. Report what must be reported, investigate to root cause, close corrective actions, and communicate what changed. That cycle, repeated honestly, is what regulators look for and what actually protects people.

Finally, remember that investigations exist alongside claims, not instead of them. A worker injured on the job may be entitled to benefits regardless of fault, and the claims process should proceed promptly and compassionately while the investigation addresses prevention. Employers who handle both tracks well, the care track and the prevention track, protect the injured worker and the operation at the same time. Neither track should wait for the other.
