Developing a Health & Safety Program That Meets WorkSafeBC Expectations

The nine core elements every effective occupational health and safety program needs — and how we build them into your custom safety plan.

Quick answer: WorkSafeBC expects every employer's health and safety program to include a signed health and safety policy, regular workplace inspections, written safe work procedures for high-risk tasks, worker orientation, education and training, a joint committee or worker representative, incident investigations, up-to-date records, and an annual program review. Alcantara Safety Solutions builds every one of these elements into your custom safety plan.

Every workplace is different, but WorkSafeBC's guidance on developing a health and safety program identifies key elements common to all effective programs. Whether you employ ten people or two hundred, these are the building blocks an inspector will look for — and the foundation of every safety plan we write. Below is each element, what it means in practice, and how we deliver it.

1. A signed health and safety policy

Your policy documents your commitment as an employer to the program and to your workers. It should state what you want the program to achieve — preventing injuries, minimizing risks, keeping employees healthy, complying with law and policy, and continually improving — and set out the rights and responsibilities of the employer, supervisors, and workers.

WorkSafeBC expects the policy to be a few clear paragraphs, signed by the CEO or senior on-site manager, dated, reviewed annually, and shared with every worker — including a copy for all new employees and a posted copy in the workplace. How we help: we draft a policy that reflects how your business actually operates, formatted for posting and onboarding packages.

2. Regular workplace inspections

Regular inspections of your workplace, equipment, and work processes are a required part of your program. Inspections by you or a supervisor identify new hazards so risks can be eliminated or controlled as they arise. How we help: we build inspection schedules, checklists, and reporting forms matched to your sites and equipment, so inspections happen on time and findings lead to corrective action.

3. Written safe work procedures

At a minimum, you must have written procedures describing how to safely carry out high-risk or complex tasks — lockout and confined space entry are the classic examples. Developing one follows four steps: define the task, break it into basic steps, identify the hazards of each step and how to eliminate or minimize them, then write the procedure workers must follow.

Not every task needs a written procedure. WorkSafeBC recommends weighing the OHS Regulation requirements, hazard level, number and experience of workers, frequency of the work, severity of potential injuries, and any inspection or investigation recommendations — and consulting your joint committee or worker representative and the workers who do the job. Procedures should list required PPE, when it must be used, and where it's found, and be reviewed whenever a job changes, new equipment is introduced, or workers return after a long absence. How we help: we facilitate the task breakdown with your crew, write the procedures in plain language, and set the review triggers.

4. Worker orientation

You must provide or review orientation when workers start a new job, move to a new worksite or department, face new hazards such as new equipment, or perform new tasks. A compliant orientation covers rights and responsibilities, demonstrates the work, shows where first aid and emergency exits are, confirms understanding with follow-up supervision, and is documented — with a copy of the orientation checklist given to the worker. How we help: we supply a complete orientation package — checklist, demonstration script, and sign-off record — tailored to your sites.

5. Education and training

Education is formal instruction; training is hands-on, job-specific demonstration with active participation so a supervisor can confirm workers understand the procedures. Supervisors carry key duties: instructing on safe procedures, observing workers daily, ensuring protective equipment is used properly, enforcing the rules, and leading crew talks. Maintain an education and training record for every worker listing dates and topics. How we help: we map required training by role, provide toolbox talk topics, and set up the record-keeping system inspectors ask for.

6. Joint committee or worker representative

Programs include either a worker health and safety representative or a joint health and safety committee that meets at least monthly, working with you to identify and resolve unsafe conditions and practices. Formal programs also require management meetings to review safety activities and incident trends — with decisions communicated back to supervisors and workers promptly. How we help: we set up meeting cadences, agendas, minute templates, and escalation paths so nothing stalls.

7. Incident investigations

After any incident requiring medical treatment — and any potentially serious near miss — you must investigate why it happened, involving at least one worker representative. The goals: determine the cause, identify contributing unsafe conditions or procedures, find ways to prevent recurrence, and prepare an investigation report, filing it with WorkSafeBC where required. Certain serious incidents — a serious injury or death, a building collapse, or a major release of a hazardous substance — must be reported to WorkSafeBC immediately. How we help: we build your investigation procedure, report templates, and immediate-reporting protocol so your team responds correctly under pressure.

8. Health and safety records

Keeping up-to-date records is essential for spotting repeat problems and providing training material. WorkSafeBC expects you to keep: program reviews, worker orientation records, inspection reports, health and safety meeting records, incident investigation reports, and first aid records. How we help: your safety plan includes a records index — what to keep, where, and for how long — so documentation is inspection-ready at any moment.

9. Annual program review

At least once a year, review your program with your workers and look for changes in the workplace that affect health and safety, including new hazards — confirming you remain compliant with the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation. How we help: we schedule and facilitate your annual review, update the plan, and document the improvements.

From requirements to a working program

Knowing the elements is one thing; making them operate daily across real crews, sites, and deadlines is another. That's the difference between a binder and a program.
