The Hierarchy of Controls: A Practical Guide for BC Employers

The hierarchy of controls is the foundation of hazard management. Learn how to apply it in your BC workplace to reduce risk effectively.

The hierarchy of controls is the framework that governs how workplace hazards should be managed. It is not a suggestion. It is the approach that WorkSafeBC inspectors expect to see when they evaluate your hazard control measures.

Despite its importance, many employers do not fully understand the hierarchy or how to apply it. This article explains the hierarchy of controls and how to use it in your BC workplace.

What the Hierarchy of Controls Is

The hierarchy of controls is a ranking of hazard control methods from most effective to least effective. The principle is simple: use the most effective method available before resorting to less effective methods.

The hierarchy has five levels:

  1. Elimination
  2. Substitution
  3. Engineering Controls
  4. Administrative Controls
  5. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Each level is less effective than the one above it. Elimination is the most effective because it removes the hazard entirely. PPE is the least effective because it relies on individual worker behavior and provides no protection if it fails or is not used.

Level 1: Elimination

Elimination means physically removing the hazard. This is always the preferred method when it is feasible.

Examples of elimination:

  • Removing a hazardous chemical from your process entirely.
  • Doing work at ground level instead of at height.
  • Discontinuing a process that is too dangerous to control.

Elimination is not always possible. You cannot eliminate the hazard of working at heights if your business is roofing. But you can often eliminate specific hazards within a process.

Level 2: Substitution

Substitution means replacing a hazardous substance or process with a less hazardous one. It is the next best option when elimination is not possible.

Examples of substitution:

  • Replacing a toxic solvent with a water-based alternative.
  • Using a quieter piece of equipment.
  • Switching from a hazardous chemical to a less hazardous one.

Substitution requires careful evaluation. The substitute must actually be less hazardous. Sometimes a substitute creates new hazards that were not present before.

Level 3: Engineering Controls

Engineering controls isolate workers from the hazard through physical means. They do not rely on worker behavior to be effective.

Examples of engineering controls:

  • Machine guards that prevent contact with moving parts.
  • Ventilation systems that remove airborne contaminants.
  • Guardrails that prevent falls.
  • Sound enclosures that reduce noise exposure.

Engineering controls are highly effective because they work automatically. They do not require workers to remember to use them.

Level 4: Administrative Controls

Administrative controls change how work is done to reduce exposure to the hazard. They rely on worker behavior and therefore are less reliable than engineering controls.

Examples of administrative controls:

  • Job rotation to limit exposure to repetitive tasks.
  • Work procedures that specify safe work methods.
  • Warning signs and labels.
  • Training programs.
  • Scheduling work to minimize exposure.

Administrative controls are important, but they should not be the primary control method when more effective options are available.

Level 5: Personal Protective Equipment

PPE is the last line of defense. It protects individual workers but does nothing to reduce the hazard itself. PPE is only acceptable when higher-level controls are not feasible or as a supplement to higher-level controls.

Examples of PPE:

  • Hard hats.
  • Safety glasses.
  • High-visibility clothing.
  • Respirators.
  • Fall arrest harnesses.

PPE must be properly selected, fitted, maintained, and used. It requires training and enforcement. And it provides no protection if it is not worn.

How to Apply the Hierarchy

Applying the hierarchy is a systematic process.

Step 1: Identify the Hazard

You cannot control a hazard you have not identified. Conduct a thorough hazard assessment to identify all hazards in your workplace.

Step 2: Consider Elimination

For each hazard, ask first whether it can be eliminated. This requires creative thinking. Can the work be done differently? Can a hazardous material be removed from the process?

Step 3: Consider Substitution

If elimination is not possible, can the hazard be substituted with something less hazardous? Evaluate substitutes carefully to ensure they do not introduce new hazards.

Step 4: Consider Engineering Controls

If substitution is not possible, can the hazard be isolated through engineering controls? This often requires investment but provides reliable protection.

Step 5: Add Administrative Controls

Administrative controls supplement higher-level controls. Develop procedures, provide training, and implement work practices that reduce exposure.

Step 6: Provide PPE

PPE is the final layer. Provide appropriate PPE for all remaining hazards. Train workers on its use and enforce its use.

Common Mistakes

These are the most common mistakes in applying the hierarchy of controls:

Jumping Straight to PPE

Many employers go directly to PPE because it is cheap and familiar. This is a mistake. PPE is the least effective control method. Always consider higher-level options first.

Ineffective Engineering Controls

Installing an engineering control that does not actually work is worse than not installing one. Engineering controls must be properly designed, installed, and maintained.

Administrative Controls Without Enforcement

Procedures that are not enforced are useless. If workers routinely bypass a procedure, the administrative control has failed.

No Review

Hazard controls must be reviewed regularly. What worked last year may not work this year if conditions have changed.

The Hierarchy in Practice

Let us look at a practical example. A warehouse has a noise hazard from a packaging machine.

  • Elimination: Can the noisy machine be removed? If packaging can be done another way, this is the best option.
  • Substitution: Can a quieter machine be used? Modern machines are often significantly quieter than older ones.
  • Engineering: Can the machine be enclosed? Can sound-absorbing materials be installed?
  • Administrative: Can workers be rotated to limit exposure time? Can the machine be scheduled for times when fewer workers are present?
  • PPE: Provide hearing protection for workers who must be near the machine.

The most effective solution is usually a combination of engineering controls and administrative controls, with PPE as a supplement.

Why Order Matters

The hierarchy ranks controls by reliability. Elimination removes the hazard entirely, so nothing depends on human behaviour. Substitution replaces the hazard with something less dangerous. Engineering controls isolate people from the hazard through design. Administrative controls change how people work. PPE protects the individual worker as the final barrier.

Each step down the hierarchy adds dependence on consistent human performance. PPE fails when it is not worn, not fitted, or not maintained. Administrative procedures fail under production pressure. Engineering controls, properly designed, protect workers even on bad days. This is why regulators and safety professionals push employers to exhaust the upper levels before settling for the lower ones.

Worked Example: A Hazardous Dust

Consider a process that generates hazardous dust. Elimination asks whether the task is needed at all. Substitution asks whether a different material or process could produce the same result without the dust. Engineering controls might enclose the process and add local exhaust ventilation, capturing dust at the source. Administrative controls would set cleaning schedules, safe work procedures, and training. PPE, respirators and eye protection, protects the worker when the other layers are insufficient.

A program that jumps straight to respirators has skipped four levels of better protection. A program that works the hierarchy from the top delivers a safer outcome and often a cheaper one, because managing exposures downstream is expensive and never-ending.

Applying the Hierarchy in Practice

Start every hazard assessment with the same question: can this hazard be removed? Most cannot be eliminated entirely, but the exercise often reveals substitution or engineering options that were never considered because the default was PPE. Document the options considered and the reasons for the final selection. This record shows inspectors, clients, and your own committee that your controls were chosen deliberately rather than by habit.

Common Mistakes in Control Selection

The most common mistake is stopping at PPE because it is fast and cheap to issue. The second most common is writing administrative procedures for hazards that engineering could remove. A third is treating substitution as impossible without testing whether alternatives exist. Each mistake has the same root: the hierarchy was never worked deliberately, so the default options won by default.

Another mistake is layering controls without removing lower ones. Adding a guard while keeping the old bypass habit, or adding ventilation while leaving the process open, produces complexity without proportionate protection. Good control design simplifies. Each added layer should either remove a residual risk or back up a layer that could realistically fail.

The Hierarchy in Your Hazard Assessments

Make the hierarchy a required field in your hazard assessment forms. For each identified hazard, the assessor documents options considered at each level and the rationale for the selected controls. This small structural change transforms assessments from checklists into decision records. Over time, the collected assessments show patterns: which controls your operations rely on most, and where engineering investment would reduce dependence on behaviour. That visibility is the strategic value of the hierarchy, beyond any single hazard it fixes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PPE always the last resort? It is the last layer, but not always unnecessary. Many hazards retain residual risk after higher controls, and PPE covers that remainder. The error is reaching for PPE first rather than last.

What if elimination is impossible? Work down the hierarchy honestly. Substitution and engineering controls resolve more hazards than most employers initially assume, and even partial improvement at higher levels reduces dependence on behaviour.

Who decides which control to use? The employer, informed by hazard assessment and often by professional advice. The decision should be documented with options considered, because that record demonstrates due diligence.

Does the hierarchy apply to office work? Yes, though the hazards are subtler. Ergonomic design, lighting, and noise control are engineering and administrative controls that precede individual accommodations.
