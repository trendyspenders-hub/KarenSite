What Is a WorkSafeBC Safety Plan (and Who Actually Needs One)?

The term gets used loosely. Here is what the law actually requires, what a real safety plan contains, and how to know if yours is adequate.

If you run a business in British Columbia and employ workers, you are legally required to have a health and safety program. Most people call this a "safety plan." The Workers Compensation Act and the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation set out what that program must contain, and WorkSafeBC inspectors use those requirements as their checklist when they walk onto your site.

The problem is that many employers do not know what a compliant program actually looks like. They download a template, fill in their company name, and assume they are covered. When an inspector arrives, the gaps between the binder and the workplace become obvious very quickly. This article explains what a WorkSafeBC safety plan is, what it must include, and how to tell if yours will hold up.

What the Law Actually Says

Section 31 of the Workers Compensation Act requires every employer to establish and maintain a health and safety program. The size and complexity of that program must reflect the size and complexity of your operation. A five-person landscaping crew does not need the same documentation as a 200-person industrial facility, but both need a written program.

Part 3 of the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation (OHSR) expands on this. It requires employers to have written safe work procedures for tasks that carry risk, to train workers on those procedures, to conduct regular inspections, and to maintain records of all of it. The Regulation also requires a Joint Health and Safety Committee (or a worker representative) once you reach 20 or more employees.

None of this is optional. WorkSafeBC issues orders for missing or inadequate programs, and those orders carry penalties. More importantly, an inadequate program leaves your workers exposed and your business liable.

What a Real Safety Plan Contains

A WorkSafeBC-compliant safety plan is not a policy binder that sits on a shelf. It is a working system. At minimum, it must include the following elements.

1. Health and Safety Policy

A signed statement from senior management committing to a safe workplace. This is the foundation. It sets the tone and establishes accountability. It must be posted where workers can see it.

2. Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment

You must systematically identify the hazards in your workplace and assess the risk each one poses. This is not a one-time exercise. It must happen before work starts, when conditions change, and after any incident. The output is a risk register that drives everything else in your program.

3. Safe Work Procedures

Written, step-by-step instructions for tasks your crews perform. These must reflect how the work is actually done, not how someone thinks it should be done. If your procedure says one thing and your workers do another, an inspector will notice.

4. Worker Training and Orientation Records

You must document that every worker has been oriented to the workplace and trained on the procedures relevant to their job. This includes new workers, young workers (under 25), and workers returning to a job after an absence. Training records are one of the first things an inspector asks for.

5. Inspection Schedule

A schedule for regular workplace inspections, along with records of what was found and what was done about it. These inspections must be documented and the corrective actions tracked to completion.

6. Incident Reporting and Investigation

A process for reporting incidents, investigating them to find root causes, and implementing corrective actions. This is required under Part 5 of the OHSR. The investigation must focus on the system failure, not on blaming the worker.

7. Emergency Preparedness

Plans for fires, medical emergencies, spills, and evacuations. Workers must know these plans and practice them. Fire drills are not optional for most workplaces.

8. First Aid

Adequate first aid supplies, trained first aid attendants, and clear procedures for getting injured workers to medical care. Part 3 of the OHSR sets out the specific requirements based on your workforce size and risk level.

9. Joint Health and Safety Committee or Worker Representative

Once you reach 20 or more employees, you must have a Joint Health and Safety Committee with trained members. Below 20, you need a worker health and safety representative. The committee must meet regularly and keep minutes.

Who Needs a Formal Program vs. a Less Formal One

WorkSafeBC distinguishes between formal programs and less formal ones. A formal program is required for employers with 20 or more workers, or for high-risk workplaces regardless of size. Less formal programs are acceptable for small, low-risk operations, but they still must be written and they still must cover the elements above.

The key word is "adequate." A program that looks good on paper but does not match your actual operations will not protect you. Inspectors compare the binder to the floor. If your safe work procedure says workers must use a harness above 6 feet but your crew is working unprotected at 12 feet, the binder does not matter.

The Template Problem

Templates are a reasonable starting point. They give you a structure. But they describe a generic business, not yours. If you use a template, you must customize every section to reflect your actual hazards, equipment, procedures, and people. This takes time and knowledge that many business owners do not have.

A custom safety plan, written from scratch around your operation, is more likely to pass inspection and more likely to actually protect your workers. It costs more upfront than a template, but the cost of a failed inspection or a workplace incident is far higher.

How to Know If Your Plan Is Adequate

Ask yourself these questions:

  • Does every safe work procedure match how the work is actually done on site?
  • Can you produce training records for every worker within five minutes?
  • Are your inspection records current and do they show corrective actions?
  • Have you updated the plan in the last 12 months, or after any significant change?
  • Would a WorkSafeBC inspector find the same conditions your binder describes?

If you answered no to any of these, your program has gaps. Those gaps are exactly what inspectors look for.

What Happens If You Do Not Have a Plan

WorkSafeBC can issue orders requiring you to produce a program, and they can impose penalties for non-compliance. In serious cases, they can stop work. Beyond the regulatory consequences, an inadequate program increases your risk of workplace incidents, which can lead to serious injury, liability, and increased WorkSafeBC premiums.

What Inspectors Look For First

When a WorkSafeBC prevention officer arrives, they usually ask for the same small set of documents before anything else. They want to see your written health and safety program or safety plan, your latest hazard assessment, your incident investigation procedure, and proof of new worker orientation. If you can produce those quickly, the inspection starts on the right foot. If you cannot, the officer knows within the first ten minutes that your program exists mostly on paper, or not at all.

Employers are often surprised that the officer spends more time in the yard, the shop, or the field than in the office. That is deliberate. The written plan is checked against what workers actually do. A procedure that no one has read or followed is treated as if it does not exist. This is why the plan has to be built around your real operations, not copied from a template.

Common Gaps We See in Existing Plans

Over the years, the same problems show up again and again in safety plans written without proper guidance:

  • Hazard assessments that were completed once and never updated, even though the work changed.
  • Emergency procedures that list phone numbers no one has dialled in years.
  • Training records that cannot be matched to actual workers on site.
  • Safe work procedures for equipment the company no longer owns.
  • A joint committee or worker representative requirement that was ignored because no one knew the thresholds.

None of these gaps require bad intent. They usually come from a plan that was assembled quickly to satisfy a client or an order, then filed away. The problem is that WorkSafeBC does not grade intent. The plan either works or it does not.

How to Test Your Own Plan

Here is a practical exercise. Give your safety plan to a supervisor who was not involved in writing it, and ask them to find the procedure for one common task, such as lifting, machine guarding, or working near traffic. Time how long it takes. Then ask a worker on site to describe how they would handle the same task. If the written procedure and the worker's answer do not match, you have found a gap worth fixing before an inspector finds it for you.

You can run the same test for emergency contacts, first aid arrangements, and incident reporting. Each mismatch is a small repair. Together they are the difference between a program that protects your workers and your business, and one that only looks good in a binder.
