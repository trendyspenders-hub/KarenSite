Managing Contractor Safety on Multi-Employer BC Worksites

Multi-employer worksites create complex safety obligations. Learn how to manage contractor safety and meet your legal duties under BC law.

Multi-employer worksites are common in BC construction, and they create complex safety challenges. When several employers share a site, who is responsible for safety? The answer is that everyone has responsibilities, and the prime contractor has specific legal duties.

This article explains how to manage contractor safety on multi-employer BC worksites.

The Legal Framework

Under BC's Workers Compensation Act, the prime contractor on a multi-employer worksite has overall responsibility for coordinating health and safety. This includes ensuring that all workers on site, regardless of who employs them, are protected.

Each employer retains responsibility for their own workers. But the prime contractor must coordinate the overall safety effort. This is a significant legal duty with serious consequences for failure.

Who Is the Prime Contractor

The prime contractor is the person or company with primary responsibility for the work on site. This is usually the general contractor, but it can be another party depending on the contract structure. The key factor is who has control over the work.

If you hire subcontractors, you may be the prime contractor. If you are a subcontractor, you have duties to your own workers and to coordinate with the prime contractor.

The Prime Contractor's Duties

The prime contractor must:

Coordinate Safety Activities

The prime contractor must coordinate the safety activities of all employers on site. This means ensuring that safety plans are shared, that hazards are communicated, and that work is sequenced safely.

Establish Site Safety Rules

The prime contractor must establish site safety rules that apply to all workers. These rules must be communicated to all employers and enforced consistently.

Conduct Site Inspections

The prime contractor must conduct regular site inspections that cover all work areas. These inspections must be documented and corrective actions tracked.

Ensure Compliance

The prime contractor must ensure that all employers on site comply with the OHS Regulation. This includes reviewing subcontractor safety programs and taking action when non-compliance is found.

Managing Subcontractor Safety

Even if you are not the prime contractor, you have duties when you hire subcontractors. Here is how to manage subcontractor safety effectively.

Pre-Qualification

Before hiring a subcontractor, assess their safety capability. Review their safety program, their incident history, their training records, and their certifications. Only hire subcontractors who meet your standards.

Orientation

Every subcontractor worker must be oriented to your site before starting work. This orientation must cover site-specific hazards, emergency procedures, and site safety rules.

Communication

Establish clear communication channels between your team and subcontractors. This includes regular safety meetings, hazard notifications, and incident reporting.

Monitoring

Monitor subcontractor work to ensure it meets safety standards. This includes regular inspections and corrective action when needed.

Enforcement

Have a process for addressing non-compliance. This should include progressive discipline and, if necessary, termination of the subcontractor contract.

Common Multi-Employer Hazards

Multi-employer worksites have specific hazards that single-employer sites do not.

Overlapping Work

Different trades working in the same area create hazards. A roofer working above a electrician creates a falling object hazard. Coordination is required to sequence work safely.

Inconsistent Safety Standards

Different employers may have different safety standards. This creates confusion and inconsistency. The prime contractor's site rules must be the standard.

Communication Gaps

Information may not flow between employers. A hazard identified by one trade may not be communicated to others. Regular safety meetings and clear communication protocols are essential.

Emergency Response

Emergency response on a multi-employer site is more complex. All workers must know the emergency procedures, regardless of who employs them. Emergency drills should include all workers on site.

Building a Contractor Safety Program

A contractor safety program should include the following elements:

  • Pre-qualification criteria and process.
  • Site orientation requirements.
  • Site safety rules.
  • Communication protocols.
  • Inspection and monitoring procedures.
  • Enforcement and termination procedures.
  • Emergency response coordination.

This program must be documented and enforced consistently.

The Consequences of Failure

Failure to manage contractor safety has serious consequences. WorkSafeBC can issue orders and penalties to the prime contractor for hazards created by subcontractors. Incidents involving subcontractor workers can result in liability for the prime contractor. And the reputational damage of a serious incident can affect your ability to win future work.

Best Practices

Here are best practices for contractor safety management:

  • Treat contractor safety with the same rigor as your own workers' safety.
  • Invest in pre-qualification. Cheap subcontractors who cut corners on safety are expensive in the long run.
  • Communicate clearly and often. Most contractor safety failures are communication failures.
  • Document everything. Inspections, orientations, and corrective actions must be recorded.
  • Enforce consistently. Rules that are not enforced are not rules.

Before the Contract Is Signed

Due diligence starts in procurement, not on site. Before awarding work, review the contractor's safety program, their WorkSafeBC registration and clearance letter, insurance certificates, and training records for key roles. For higher-risk work, ask for their hazard assessments and site-specific safety plans. The goal is not to make your business an expert on their trade. It is to confirm they run a professional operation that manages its own risks.

Document the review. If an incident later involves a contractor, one of the first questions asked will be what screening you performed before engagement.

During the Work

Once work starts, coordination becomes the duty. Host employers and prime contractors must share site-specific hazard information: underground services, overhead lines, existing chemical exposures, traffic patterns, and emergency arrangements. Orientation for visiting contractors should cover this information before work begins, with attendance documented.

Monitoring does not mean micromanaging their craft. It means verifying that agreed safety arrangements are functioning: orientations completed, hazards communicated, incidents reported, and protocols followed at interfaces where your workers and theirs interact. Interface zones deserve particular attention, because mixed-crew areas are where assumptions collide.

After the Work Ends

Close the loop with a debrief. Review any incidents, near misses, or coordination problems while they are fresh. Note what worked for future contracts. Strong contractor safety management is cumulative: each engagement refines the screening, orientation, and monitoring processes, and the improvement compounds across projects.

The Prime Contractor Question

On construction sites, the prime contractor carries specific coordination duties. If you are the prime, your obligations extend across every employer on site, not just your own crew. This includes coordinating health and safety activities, ensuring a site orientation, and establishing common emergency procedures. Failing to designate or perform the prime role properly is a common finding on multi-employer worksites, and the consequences reach the principal who hired the work.

Even when you are not the prime, your duties as a hiring employer remain. Screening, orientation of your own workers to the site, and coordination at interfaces are yours. Confusion about who is responsible is not a defence; on multi-employer sites, responsibility is shared by design.

Contract Clauses That Support Safety

Safety expectations belong in the contract, not just on the site. Useful clauses include the right to stop work for imminent hazards, documentation requirements such as clearance letters before mobilization, incident reporting timelines, and termination rights for repeated safety failures. These clauses are tools for enforcement that align commercial terms with safety expectations. Consult legal counsel to adapt them to your agreements, because enforceability matters as much as intent.

Frequently Asked Questions

Am I liable for my contractor's safety failures? Liability depends on circumstances, but your duties as a hiring employer are real. Screening, information sharing, and coordination are yours regardless of what the contract says.

What screening is enough for a small job? Proportionality applies. For low-risk work, clearance letter, insurance, and a basic program review may suffice. For higher-risk work, expect deeper review of hazard assessments and site plans.

Can I put all safety responsibility in the contract? Contract language helps, but it cannot transfer duties the law assigns to you. Both the contract and your operational practice must align with the regulation.

What if a contractor refuses to follow site rules? Escalate per the contract, up to stopping the work. Allowing known non-compliance to continue undermines your program and your defence.

The Bottom Line

Contractor safety management is shared responsibility made operational. Screen before signing, coordinate during the work, and debrief after it. Contracts should reinforce the expectations, but the practice is what protects people and positions. Employers who run this well rarely face the worst outcomes, because problems surface early, while they are still small enough to fix cheaply.

Finally, keep the relationship dimension in view. The best contractor safety arrangements rest on clear expectations delivered early and consistently, not on surprise enforcement. Contractors who know your standards before mobilization usually meet them. The few who cannot or will not are better discovered at the screening stage than mid-project, when disentangling costs far more than the original review would have.

Documentation completes the picture. Keep records of screening, orientations, coordination meetings, and any corrective actions involving contractors. These records demonstrate your due diligence and make future reviews faster. In the event of an incident involving a contractor, they also establish what you did and when, which matters greatly in the questions that follow.
