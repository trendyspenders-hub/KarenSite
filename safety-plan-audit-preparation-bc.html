How to Prepare for a Safety Plan Audit in British Columbia

Whether it is a COR audit, client audit, or WorkSafeBC inspection, preparation is key. Learn how to get your safety plan audit-ready.

Whether you are preparing for a COR audit, a client pre-qualification audit, or a WorkSafeBC inspection, the key to success is preparation. Audits are stressful, but they do not have to be painful. With proper preparation, you can approach any audit with confidence.

This article explains how to prepare for a safety plan audit in BC.

Types of Audits

Several types of audits affect BC employers.

COR Audits

COR audits are conducted by certified external auditors to verify that your safety program meets the COR standard. They are required for COR certification and maintenance.

Client Audits

Many clients conduct their own audits or hire third parties to audit their suppliers. These audits vary in scope and rigor but generally focus on program adequacy and implementation.

WorkSafeBC Inspections

WorkSafeBC inspections are not technically audits, but they serve a similar function. An inspector reviews your program and your workplace to verify compliance with the OHS Regulation.

Internal Audits

Internal audits are conducted by your own team or a consultant to identify gaps before an external audit finds them. Internal audits are a critical part of audit preparation.

What Auditors Look For

Regardless of the audit type, auditors generally look for the same things.

Program Adequacy

Does your written program meet the relevant standard? Is it complete? Is it current? Auditors will review your program document by document.

Implementation

Is the program actually implemented? Do workers follow the procedures? Are inspections conducted? Is training documented? This is where many employers fall short.

Records

Can you produce the records that prove implementation? Training records, inspection logs, meeting minutes, incident reports. Auditors will ask for these.

Worker Knowledge

Do workers know the procedures? Can they explain the hazards of their job? Auditors will interview workers to verify that training has been effective.

The Preparation Process

Here is a systematic process for preparing for an audit.

Step 1: Know the Standard

Understand what standard you are being audited against. For COR, this is the COR audit tool. For client audits, ask for their audit criteria. For WorkSafeBC, it is the OHS Regulation. Do not prepare in the dark.

Step 2: Conduct a Gap Assessment

Compare your current program against the standard. Identify gaps. This can be done internally or with a consultant. Be honest. Gaps you identify now are gaps you can fix before the audit.

Step 3: Fix the Gaps

Prioritize the gaps and fix them. Start with the most serious gaps first. Some fixes are quick. Others take time. Start early.

Step 4: Organize Your Documentation

Organize your program documents and records so they are easy to find. Auditors will ask for specific documents. Being able to produce them quickly demonstrates professionalism.

Step 5: Train Your Workers

Ensure your workers are trained on the procedures relevant to their jobs. They should be able to explain these procedures to an auditor. Conduct refresher training if needed.

Step 6: Conduct a Mock Audit

A mock audit simulates the real audit. Have someone who is not familiar with your program review it as an auditor would. This reveals gaps that internal reviews may miss.

Step 7: Prepare Your Team

Tell your team that an audit is coming. Explain what to expect. Assign someone to accompany the auditor. Ensure everyone knows their role.

Common Audit Failures

These are the most common reasons for audit failure:

Procedures That Do Not Match Practice

The written procedure says one thing. The workers do another. This is the most common audit finding and the most serious.

Missing Records

Training records, inspection logs, or meeting minutes that cannot be produced. If it is not documented, it did not happen.

Outdated Program

A program that has not been updated in over a year. Auditors check dates. An outdated program suggests a lack of commitment.

Workers Who Cannot Explain Procedures

Workers who cannot explain the procedures they are supposed to follow. This indicates that training was inadequate or ineffective.

Unaddressed Corrective Actions

Previous audit findings or inspection items that have not been addressed. This shows a lack of follow-through.

During the Audit

When the audit occurs, stay calm. Answer questions honestly. Do not guess. If you do not know something, say so and offer to find out. Provide documents promptly. Correct any hazards the auditor identifies immediately if possible.

Remember that auditors are not trying to catch you. They are trying to verify that your program works. Cooperation and honesty go a long way.

After the Audit

After the audit, review the findings. Address any corrective actions promptly. Document your responses. If you disagree with a finding, follow the appeal process. But even if you appeal, work toward compliance.

Use the audit as a learning opportunity. Every finding is a chance to improve your program.

Know Your Audit Type

Not every audit is the same, and preparation depends on which kind you face. A client pre-qualification audit screens your documentation against their vendor requirements. A COR audit evaluates your program against a certified standard. A WorkSafeBC inspection verifies regulatory compliance. A third-party safety audit, sometimes requested by insurers or corporate parents, applies its own criteria. Each has different weightings, but all of them examine the same foundation: whether your documented program matches your actual operations.

Confirm the audit standard in writing before preparation begins. Auditing against the wrong checklist wastes preparation effort and leaves real gaps untouched.

The Six-Week Preparation Plan

Start six weeks out with a documentation sweep. Pull every policy, procedure, hazard assessment, and training record into one location. Verify dates and signatures. Fix the obvious gaps immediately: expired certifications, missing orientation records, outdated emergency contacts.

At four weeks, run an internal mock audit against the standard. Walk the floor and compare conditions to procedures. Interview a few supervisors and workers informally about what they would show an auditor. Their answers reveal the distance between paper and practice.

At two weeks, close the remaining corrective actions, refresh training where needed, and organize the evidence binder or digital folder so any document can be produced within minutes. In the final days, confirm logistics: who meets the auditor, who accompanies them, and who can answer questions in each area.

Day-Of Discipline

On audit day, be present and cooperative. Answer what is asked, honestly, and produce documents promptly. Do not speculate or volunteer guesses; if you do not know, say so and find out. Workers should be encouraged to speak freely. Attempts to stage manage the audit almost always backfire, because experienced auditors detect rehearsed environments quickly. The employers who perform best in audits are simply the ones whose normal, daily operation is already audit-ready.

Common Audit Findings and How to Pre-empt Them

Across audit types, the same findings repeat. Hazard assessments that are outdated relative to current operations. Training records that cannot be matched to workers on site. Inspection findings without closed corrective actions. Emergency procedures untested in practice. Orientation completed on paper but not delivered in substance. Each of these is preventable with the preparation plan described above, because each is a gap between the documented program and the operating one.

Pre-empt them by fixing the operational reality first, then the documents. Auditors trust what they observe over what they are shown. A clean floor with honest records outperforms a polished binder with visible gaps, every time.

Using Audit Results Constructively

Whether the result is a pass, a conditional pass, or findings requiring work, the audit's value is information. Convert findings into a corrective action plan with owners and dates. Feed them into the next revision of your safety plan. Review them at committee meetings until closed. Employers who treat audits as free consulting improve steadily. Employers who treat them as adversarial events repeat the same findings year after year, and wonder why nothing changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far in advance should we start preparing? Six weeks suits most situations. If your documentation is badly out of date, allow longer. Preparation compresses poorly at the last minute.

Who should be the audit contact? One person who knows the program and can route questions quickly. Typically a safety lead or operations manager. Auditors respect efficient navigation more than extensive escorts.

Can workers talk to the auditor alone? Yes, and they should be able to. Preparing workers means encouraging honesty, not scripting answers.

What if we fail the audit? Most audit systems include corrective action periods rather than outright failure. Treat findings as a work order, close them systematically, and the next audit reflects the improvement.

The Bottom Line

Audit preparation is not theatre. It is the discipline of keeping your program audit-ready every day, then confirming it before someone else does. Six weeks of structured preparation turns a stressful event into a confirmation of how you already operate. And the findings, whatever they are, are the real deliverable: a precise list of where your program can improve next.
