What a Workplace Safety Manual Includes

"Safety manual," "safety program," and "safety plan" get used interchangeably. In practice, a manual is the written core of your health and safety program: the documents that say what your hazards are, who is responsible for what, and what workers must do. Below are the sections a practical manual typically contains for a BC employer. The exact contents are set by your hazards and size, not by a template.

1. Company commitment and responsibilities

A policy signed by ownership, a statement of responsibilities, and — depending on crew size — the structure of your joint health and safety committee or worker representative, as required by the Workers Compensation Act.

2. Hazard identification and control

The heart of the manual: a register of your hazards, the risk assessments behind them, and the controls in place. New hazards must be added as work changes; a manual frozen at day one is the most common failure auditors flag.

3. Safe work procedures

Step-by-step procedures for your higher-risk recurring tasks, written for the worker performing them — short sentences, specific to your equipment, not copied generic text.

4. Training and orientation records

What training is required, who has received it, and when it expires. Regulators ask for records, not intentions.

5. Incident reporting and investigation

How workers report injuries, near misses, and unsafe conditions; how investigations are conducted; and what gets reported to WorkSafeBC and when.

6. Emergency preparedness

Site-specific emergency procedures, muster points, first aid arrangements, and roles.

7. Program administration

Who owns each document, the review schedule, and how version control works so nobody on site works from a superseded page.

What a manual is not

A manual is not a binder that sits in a truck. It is a living system: the written part, plus the training, records, and reviews that make it true on the ground.
