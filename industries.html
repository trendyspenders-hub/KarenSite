Industry-Specific Safety Planning

Why Your Industry Determines Your Safety Plan — Not Just Your Company Size

The short answer: every industry has its own injury profile, its own most-cited regulations, and its own inspection culture. A safety plan that works for a construction site will fail a healthcare audit — and vice versa. Effective safety planning starts with the hazards and standards specific to your sector, then gets tailored further to your facility.

Two businesses with fifty employees can face completely different regulatory worlds. That's why we organize our work by industry first — and why a generic plan is so dangerous. Below is what actually matters in each sector we serve.

Construction: Falls, the Fatal Four, and Multi-Employer Liability

Construction consistently records the highest number of workplace fatalities of any industry, and the "Fatal Four" — falls, struck-by incidents, electrocution, and caught-in hazards — account for the majority. But the compliance challenge goes deeper: construction sites are multi-employer environments where the prime contractor can be held responsible for subcontractor failures. A construction safety plan must cover site-specific fall protection, orientation documentation for a rotating workforce, and coordination procedures that protect you when other companies' workers step onto your site.

Manufacturing: Machine Guarding, Lockout/Tagout, and Chemical Programs

Manufacturing injuries are less frequent but more severe — amputations, crushing injuries, and chemical exposures dominate the claims data. Control of hazardous energy (lockout/tagout under OSHA 1910.147) is one of the most-cited standards in all of general industry, usually because written procedures don't match actual machines. Machine guarding assessments, HazCom/WHMIS programs, and hearing conservation round out the core requirements for most plants.

Warehousing & Logistics: Forklifts, Racking, and the Tempo Problem

Warehouse safety has one defining challenge: speed pressure. Quota-driven operations create an environment where workers take shortcuts precisely where the hazards are worst — forklift-pedestrian interactions, loading docks, and racking systems. An effective warehouse safety program has to work at operational tempo: traffic management plans, rapid onboarding for seasonal labor, racking inspection routines, and near-miss reporting that doesn't punish honesty.

Healthcare: Protecting the People Who Provide Care

Healthcare workers experience injury and illness rates above those in construction and manufacturing — driven by patient handling, workplace violence, needle sticks, and biological exposure. Care settings also carry layered obligations: OHS regulations on top of licensing and accreditation requirements. A healthcare safety plan must address safe patient handling programs, violence prevention protocols, exposure control plans, and hazardous drug handling — with training that works around 24/7 shift coverage.

Food Service, Oil & Gas, and Beyond

Food service operations face burns, cuts, slips, and chemical hazards compounded by high turnover — meaning training systems matter more than manuals. Oil & gas and logistics operations face high-consequence, low-frequency hazards: confined spaces, H2S exposure, driving incidents, and contractor management across remote sites. Different worlds, same principle: the plan must match the real hazard profile.

The Regulatory Landscape Is Different in Every Sector

Inspectors specialize. A construction inspector expects fall protection plans and prime contractor documentation. A general industry inspector goes straight to lockout/tagout and HazCom. Healthcare reviewers look for exposure control and violence prevention. Knowing what your inspectors will ask for — before they arrive — is half the value of an industry-specific program. Every plan we build is written against the standards that apply to your sector and your jurisdiction, whether that's federal and state OSHA requirements or the WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation.

How We Tailor a Plan to Your Industry

Our process starts with your industry's hazard profile and citation history, then narrows to your facility. The on-site assessment identifies which sector-typical hazards actually exist in your operation, which controls you already have, and where the real gaps are. The result is a plan that's industry-correct at the framework level and facility-specific at the procedure level — the combination that passes inspections and, more importantly, prevents injuries.
