OSHA vs. WorkSafeBC

The key differences between U.S. and B.C. workplace safety law — and how one well-built program can satisfy both.

Cross-Border Consultation WorkSafeBC Program Guide
Cross-Border Compliance

The Short Answer

OSHA governs workplace safety in the United States under federal law (with more than 20 states running their own OSHA-approved plans). WorkSafeBC governs it in British Columbia under the provincial Workers Compensation Act and OHS Regulation. The systems overlap about 80% in substance but differ in structure: BC mandates formal program elements and joint worker committees that federal OSHA does not, while OSHA's penalty tiers and citation system have no direct BC equivalent. Businesses operating in both need a program deliberately built for both.

Side-by-Side: What Actually Differs

Area OSHA (United States) WorkSafeBC (British Columbia)
Legal basis Federal OSH Act; 20+ states run approved state plans (e.g., California, Washington) with equal or stricter rules Workers Compensation Act + OHS Regulation, administered by WorkSafeBC
Written program Specific written programs required (hazard communication, lockout/tagout, emergency action, etc.) but no single overarching program mandate federally Formal health & safety program required for employers with 20+ workers; smaller employers still need an informal program
Worker participation No general federal joint committee requirement Joint health & safety committee mandatory at 20+ workers; worker representative at 10–19
Enforcement Citation system; penalties now exceed $16,500 per serious violation and $165,000 for willful/repeated, adjusted annually Orders + administrative penalties that can reach well into six figures for serious or repeat non-compliance
Recognition programs Voluntary Protection Programs (VPP) Certificate of Recognition (COR) — often required to bid on BC contracts

Which Rules Apply to You?

Jurisdiction follows the work, not the head office. A BC company sending a crew to a Washington job site owes OSHA compliance for that work; a U.S. company operating in BC falls under WorkSafeBC. If your business touches both, you need both — and the regulators don't accept the other country's paperwork as a substitute.

Building One Program for Both

The efficient architecture is a common core with jurisdiction modules. The core covers what both systems demand: management commitment and policy, defined responsibilities, hazard identification and control, training and orientation, inspections, incident investigation, and records. The modules then layer on the divergent pieces — joint committee procedures and COR alignment for BC; hazard communication, lockout/tagout documentation, and 300-log recordkeeping for OSHA.

Done this way, an audit in either jurisdiction finds a program that speaks its language. Done the other way — one country's plan stretched to cover both — both auditors find gaps. Cross-border programs are a specialty of ours; if your operation spans the border, that's exactly what our custom safety plan service builds.

Where Businesses Get Caught

Three patterns account for most cross-border failures we see: assuming an OSHA-compliant plan passes in BC (it won't — no joint committee, wrong program structure); assuming a COR-certified BC program satisfies OSHA (it won't — missing required written programs and recordkeeping); and treating the second jurisdiction as an afterthought bolted on later. The fix in every case is the same: one program, two modules, built together.
