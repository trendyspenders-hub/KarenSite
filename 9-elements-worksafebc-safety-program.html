The 9 Elements of a WorkSafeBC Health and Safety Program

Every BC health and safety program must cover these 9 elements. Learn what each one requires and how to implement them in your workplace.

WorkSafeBC requires every employer in British Columbia to maintain a health and safety program. The specific content of that program depends on the size and complexity of your operation, but certain core elements are required regardless of industry or headcount.

Whether you are building a program from scratch or auditing an existing one, these are the nine elements every compliant program must address.

1. Health and Safety Policy

A health and safety policy is a signed commitment from senior management to maintain a safe workplace. It establishes the principle that safety is a core business value, not an afterthought. The policy must be written, signed by the most senior person in the organization, posted where workers can see it, and reviewed annually.

A good policy is short and direct. It states the employer's commitment to compliance, to worker participation, and to continuous improvement. It does not need to be long. One page is enough.

2. Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment

You cannot control hazards you have not identified. A systematic hazard identification process is the foundation of your entire program. This means regularly walking your workplace to identify physical hazards, reviewing incident reports to identify trends, and consulting workers about the risks they face.

Once identified, each hazard must be assessed for risk. How likely is it to cause harm? How severe would the harm be? The output is a prioritized risk register that drives your control measures.

This is not a one-time exercise. Hazard assessments must be updated when conditions change, when new equipment or processes are introduced, and after any incident.

3. Safe Work Procedures

Safe work procedures are written, step-by-step instructions for tasks that carry risk. They must be specific to your operation and must reflect how the work is actually done. Generic procedures that describe work your crew does not perform are useless.

Every high-risk task needs a procedure. This includes tasks like working at heights, confined space entry, lockout, excavation, and operating heavy equipment. Workers must be trained on the procedures relevant to their job.

4. Worker Training and Orientation

Training is how you ensure workers can do their jobs safely. Every worker needs orientation before they start work. Orientation covers the workplace layout, emergency procedures, hazard reporting, and the worker's rights and responsibilities.

Beyond orientation, workers need task-specific training. A forklift operator needs different training than a flagger. Training must be documented. Records should include the date, content, trainer, and worker name.

5. Workplace Inspections

Regular workplace inspections catch hazards before they cause incidents. Inspections should be scheduled, documented, and followed up. The person conducting the inspection must have the knowledge to identify hazards relevant to your operation.

Records of inspections must include what was found, what was done about it, and when it was done. Inspectors will ask to see these records.

6. Incident Reporting and Investigation

Incidents, including near misses, must be reported and investigated. The purpose of investigation is not to assign blame. It is to find the root cause and prevent recurrence.

A good investigation asks why the incident happened, not just what happened. It looks at the system failures that allowed the incident to occur. Was the procedure inadequate? Was training insufficient? Was equipment poorly maintained?

Investigation reports must be documented and corrective actions tracked to completion.

7. Emergency Preparedness

Every workplace needs plans for emergencies. This includes fires, medical emergencies, chemical spills, natural disasters, and evacuations. Plans must be written, workers must be trained on them, and drills must be conducted regularly.

Emergency equipment must be maintained. Fire extinguishers must be serviced. First aid kits must be stocked. Emergency exits must be clear and marked.

8. First Aid

The OHS Regulation sets out specific first aid requirements based on workforce size and risk level. This includes the number of trained first aid attendants, the level of training they need, the supplies you must stock, and the procedures for getting injured workers to medical care.

First aid certificates must be current. Supplies must be checked regularly. Attendants must know their role.

9. Joint Health and Safety Committee or Worker Representative

Workplaces with 20 or more employees must have a Joint Health and Safety Committee (JHSC). The committee must include worker and employer representatives, meet at least monthly, and keep minutes. Committee members must receive training.

Workplaces with fewer than 20 employees need a worker health and safety representative. The representative has similar rights and responsibilities to a JHSC but operates individually.

How These Elements Work Together

These nine elements are not independent. They form a system. Your hazard assessments inform your safe work procedures. Your procedures drive your training. Your inspections verify that procedures are followed. Your incident investigations reveal gaps in your procedures or training. Your JHSC reviews all of it and recommends improvements.

When one element is weak, the whole system suffers. A program with excellent procedures but poor training is ineffective. A program with great training but no inspections is blind to emerging hazards.

How to Audit Your Program

Review each element against your current practice. Ask:

  • Is our policy current and posted?
  • Do our hazard assessments match our actual workplace?
  • Do our procedures reflect how work is actually done?
  • Can we produce training records for every worker?
  • Are our inspections happening on schedule with documented follow-up?
  • Are we investigating incidents to root cause?
  • Are our emergency plans current and practiced?
  • Are our first aid supplies and certificates current?
  • Is our JHSC or worker representative active and trained?

If you answered no to any of these, you have a gap. Gaps are what inspectors find.

How the Nine Elements Connect to Each Other

One mistake employers make is treating the nine elements as nine separate assignments. They are not. Each element feeds the next. Hazard identification tells you what your safe work procedures must cover. The procedures determine what training workers need. Training records prove orientation happened. Committee meetings review inspection results, which restart the hazard identification cycle. When one element is missing, the whole chain weakens.

This is why adding procedures without training, or training without records, does not produce a functioning program. WorkSafeBC officers look for the connections. They will trace a single hazard from identification, through the procedure, into the training, and onto the floor. A break anywhere in that chain is treated as a program failure, even if every individual document looks fine on its own.

Prioritizing When You Cannot Fix Everything at Once

If your program has gaps, fix them in an order that reduces risk fastest:

  • Start with hazard identification and safe work procedures for your highest-risk activities.
  • Make sure every worker, especially new and young workers, receives documented orientation before starting work.
  • Confirm your first aid arrangements match your workforce size and remote access considerations.
  • Set up the joint committee or worker representative structure if your thresholds require it.
  • Then build out the remaining documentation, inspections, and investigations.

This sequence mirrors what an inspector will prioritize. Immediate physical risks come first, followed by the structures that prevent them from returning.

Keeping the Program Alive

A written program that is never reviewed gradually stops matching reality. Workers change, equipment changes, sites change, and regulations change. Schedule an annual review at minimum, plus a review after every incident, every significant change in operations, and every order or inspection finding. Treat the program as a living tool. The employers who stay inspection-ready are not the ones with the thickest binders. They are the ones whose paperwork matches the worksite on any given day.

A Practical Self-Assessment

Score your own program against the nine elements with a simple three-point scale for each: functioning well, partially in place, or missing. Functioning means the element is documented, current, and verifiable on the floor. Partially in place means some documentation exists but the connections are weak. Missing means the element would not survive an inspector's question.

Any element scored missing is urgent. Partial elements deserve a plan and a date. This honest exercise, done annually, keeps the program from drifting. It also converts an abstract standard into a concrete to-do list, which is how real improvement happens in busy operations.

Where Employers Get Help

Building all nine elements properly takes time and specific expertise. Many employers engage a safety consultant for the build phase, then maintain the program internally. Others handle maintenance through their joint committee with periodic external reviews. Both models work. What does not work is treating the program as finished once the binder arrives. Regulation changes, operations change, and the plan must change with them. Budget for the maintenance from the start and the program stays an asset instead of becoming a liability.
