WorkSafeBC Inspection? Here Is What Happens and How to Prepare

A WorkSafeBC inspector has arrived. Learn what happens during the inspection, what they check, and how to prepare your documentation and your crew.

A WorkSafeBC inspection is one of the most stressful experiences a business owner can face. The inspector arrives unannounced, walks your floor, interviews your workers, and asks for documentation you may or may not have. Knowing what to expect and how to prepare can make the difference between a clean inspection and a costly order.

This article explains what actually happens during a WorkSafeBC inspection, what inspectors look for, and how to get ready.

Why WorkSafeBC Inspects

WorkSafeBC conducts inspections for several reasons. They inspect high-risk industries on a scheduled basis. They respond to complaints from workers or the public. They investigate incidents. They follow up on previous orders. They also conduct targeted inspections based on injury statistics or emerging hazards.

Regardless of the trigger, the process is largely the same. The inspector arrives, introduces themselves, and begins their review.

What Happens During the Inspection

Here is the typical sequence of events.

1. Opening Meeting

The inspector introduces themselves, explains the purpose of the visit, and asks to meet with a company representative. They will ask for your health and safety program, your most recent inspection records, and your training documentation. Have these ready.

2. Documentation Review

The inspector reviews your written program. They compare it against the OHS Regulation and look for gaps. They check your hazard assessments, safe work procedures, training records, inspection logs, and incident investigation reports. They are looking for completeness and currency. A program that has not been updated in two years is a red flag.

3. Floor Walk

The inspector walks your workplace. They observe workers performing tasks and compare what they see to your written procedures. They check for hazards: missing machine guards, improper storage, inadequate fall protection, missing signage, blocked exits. They take photographs and notes.

4. Worker Interviews

The inspector interviews workers privately. They ask about training, procedures, and whether workers feel safe reporting concerns. This is where the gap between your binder and your floor becomes obvious. If workers cannot explain the procedures they are supposed to follow, the inspector will note it.

5. Closing Meeting

The inspector summarizes their findings. They may issue orders if they find violations. Orders specify what must be fixed and by when. They may also issue violation tickets or administrative penalties for serious or repeat violations.

What Inspectors Look For

Inspectors have a mental checklist based on the OHS Regulation. The most common items they check are:

  • Current hazard assessments for all high-risk tasks.
  • Safe work procedures that match actual practice.
  • Training records for every worker, including orientation.
  • Regular workplace inspections with documented corrective actions.
  • Proper personal protective equipment (PPE) and enforcement.
  • Machine guarding and lockout procedures.
  • Fall protection for work above 6 feet.
  • Confined space entry procedures where applicable.
  • First aid supplies and trained attendants.
  • Emergency procedures and fire drill records.
  • Joint Health and Safety Committee meeting minutes.
  • Incident investigation reports for any recent incidents.

How to Prepare Before an Inspection

The best preparation is ongoing. If you wait until the inspector arrives, it is too late. Here is what you should have ready at all times.

Documentation

Keep your written program current and accessible. Ensure training records are complete and organized. Document every inspection and corrective action. Keep incident investigation reports on file. Have all of this in a central location that a supervisor can access within five minutes.

Worker Training

Every worker must be trained on the procedures relevant to their job. This is not a one-time event. New workers need orientation before they start work. Existing workers need refresher training when procedures change or annually at minimum.

Floor Conditions

Keep your workplace clean and organized. Remove hazards as soon as they are identified. Ensure PPE is available and used. Check that machine guards are in place and lockout procedures are followed. These are the things inspectors see first.

What to Do During an Inspection

When the inspector arrives, stay calm. Assign one person to accompany them. Answer questions honestly and directly. Do not volunteer information that was not asked for, but do not obstruct or delay the inspection. If you do not know an answer, say so and offer to find out.

If the inspector identifies a hazard, correct it immediately if possible. Document the correction. If you cannot correct it immediately, explain your plan and timeline.

If You Receive an Order

If the inspector issues an order, read it carefully. It will specify what must be fixed and the deadline. You must comply by the deadline or request an extension with justification. Failure to comply can result in stop-work orders, additional penalties, or prosecution.

If you disagree with the order, you have the right to appeal. Contact WorkSafeBC for the appeal process. In the meantime, you should still work toward compliance unless the order is stayed.

After the Inspection

Follow up on any orders by the deadline. Document your corrective actions. Update your program to prevent recurrence. If the inspection revealed systemic gaps, consider hiring a consultant to help you close them.

The Three Phases of an Inspection

Most WorkSafeBC inspections follow a recognizable pattern. Understanding the phases helps you respond calmly and appropriately.

Opening meeting. The officer introduces themselves, explains the reason for the visit, which may be a complaint, a referral, a planned inspection program, or a follow-up on a previous order. They will usually ask for your safety documentation right away. This is where organization matters. Being able to hand over your plan, orientation records, and recent inspection reports without scrambling sets the tone.

Workplace walkthrough. The officer tours the work areas and observes actual work. They may talk to workers directly, and workers have the right to speak honestly without you present. The officer is comparing what they see against what your documentation says. They look for immediate hazards first: unguarded machinery, missing fall protection, blocked exits, improper storage.

Closing meeting. The officer summarizes their findings, issues any orders or penalties, and explains your rights and the timelines for compliance. Ask questions here. If something is unclear, this is the time to clarify what corrective action is expected and by when.

What Not to Do During an Inspection

Employers sometimes make the situation worse without meaning to. Avoid these common mistakes:

  • Do not argue with the officer at the workplace. Disagreements are handled through the review process, not in the moment.
  • Do not coach workers on what to say. Officers notice rehearsed answers, and they undermine credibility.
  • Do not hide or delay access to records. Refusing entry or obstructing an inspection carries its own consequences.
  • Do not sign anything you have not read. Read orders carefully before acknowledging them.
  • Do not treat the inspection as a personal attack. The officer is documenting conditions, not judging character.

After the Inspection: Closing Orders Properly

Every order comes with a compliance deadline. Treat that deadline as immovable. Complete the corrective work, gather evidence such as photos, revised procedures, and training records, and submit the confirmation before the due date. If you genuinely cannot comply in time, contact the officer before the deadline to request an extension. Officers respond far better to early, honest communication than to silence followed by a missed date.

Keep a log of every inspection, order, and corrective action. Patterns matter. An employer with one minor order in five years is viewed very differently from one with repeated orders for the same issue.

Complaint-Driven Inspections

Many inspections begin with a worker complaint, and this changes the dynamic. The officer arrives with the complaint in hand and will verify its specific allegations. Complaints can be anonymous, and it is unlawful to retaliate against a worker for raising safety concerns. Employers who respond to complaints with suspicion or discipline create a second, more serious problem.

The practical response is the same as for any inspection: cooperate, produce documentation, correct genuine issues. If the complaint is unfounded, the inspection will show that. If it has substance, you have received valuable information. Either way, how you treat the complaining worker afterwards will be remembered by the entire workforce, and it shapes whether future hazards get reported early or hidden until someone is hurt.

Records Worth Keeping Inspection-Ready

The difference between a smooth inspection and a painful one usually comes down to records. Keep the following current and accessible: your written program, hazard assessments, orientation and training records, committee minutes, inspection reports, incident investigations, first aid records, and any orders or correspondence with WorkSafeBC. Assign one person responsibility for this file. When an officer asks, the answer should be a quick retrieval, not a search.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I refuse entry to a WorkSafeBC officer? No. Prevention officers have the legal authority to enter workplaces to carry out their duties. Refusing entry or obstructing an inspection is itself a violation that creates consequences separate from whatever they came to examine.

Will I be told who made a complaint? Not necessarily. Complaints can be made anonymously, and the identity of a worker who raised a concern is protected. The officer will verify the substance of the complaint regardless of its source.

What if I disagree with an order? You have the right to request a review within the time limits set out when the order is issued. Comply first where required, document your position, and pursue the review through the proper channel rather than debating the officer at the workplace.

How often do inspections happen? There is no fixed schedule for most employers. Inspections follow complaints, referrals, targeted inspection initiatives for high-risk sectors, and follow-ups on previous orders. Maintaining readiness continuously is far easier than scrambling when one arrives.
