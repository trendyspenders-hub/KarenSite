Common Orders and Penalties Under the Workers Compensation Act

WorkSafeBC can issue orders, violation tickets, and administrative penalties for safety violations. Learn what these are, how much they cost, and how

When WorkSafeBC finds a safety violation, they have several tools at their disposal. They can issue orders requiring corrective action. They can issue violation tickets with immediate fines. They can impose administrative penalties for serious or repeat violations. And in the most serious cases, they can pursue prosecution.

This article explains the most common orders and penalties under the Workers Compensation Act, what triggers them, and how to avoid them.

Types of Enforcement Actions

WorkSafeBC uses a graduated enforcement model. The severity of the action depends on the severity and history of the violation.

Orders

An order is a written direction from a WorkSafeBC officer requiring you to correct a violation by a specific deadline. Orders are the most common enforcement action. They can range from simple corrective actions like fixing a machine guard to complex requirements like implementing a full safety program.

Orders are not fines, but they carry consequences. Failure to comply with an order can result in stop-work orders, additional penalties, and prosecution.

Violation Tickets

Violation tickets are on-the-spot fines for specific regulatory violations. They are similar to traffic tickets. The fine amount is set by regulation and varies by violation. Common violations that result in tickets include missing PPE, inadequate fall protection, and missing machine guards.

Administrative Penalties

Administrative penalties are more serious. They are imposed for high-risk violations, repeat violations, or violations that result in serious injury. The penalty amount is based on the severity of the violation and your payroll. Penalties can range from thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Prosecution

In the most serious cases, particularly those involving fatality or serious injury, WorkSafeBC can recommend criminal prosecution. This is rare but has happened in BC. Convictions can result in significant fines and even jail time.

Common Orders and What Triggers Them

These are the orders we see most frequently in BC workplaces.

Missing or Inadequate Safety Program

This is the most common order for small to mid-sized employers. The inspector finds that the employer does not have a written safety program or that the program is inadequate for the operation. The order requires the employer to develop or update the program by a specific deadline.

Inadequate Hazard Assessment

The inspector finds that the employer has not conducted a proper hazard assessment or that the assessment does not match the actual workplace. The order requires a new or updated assessment.

Missing Safe Work Procedures

The inspector finds that workers are performing high-risk tasks without written procedures or that the procedures do not match actual practice. The order requires procedures to be developed and implemented.

Inadequate Training Documentation

The inspector cannot find training records for workers performing hazardous tasks. The order requires training to be conducted and documented.

Fall Protection Violations

Workers are working at heights without adequate fall protection. This is one of the most serious and most common violations in BC construction. It often results in both an order and a violation ticket.

Machine Guarding Violations

Machinery is not properly guarded. This is a serious hazard that can result in amputation or death. Orders for machine guarding violations are common in manufacturing and warehousing.

Confined Space Violations

Workers are entering confined spaces without proper procedures, training, or equipment. This is one of the deadliest hazards in the workplace. Confined space violations often result in serious penalties.

How Penalties Are Calculated

Administrative penalties are calculated based on several factors:

  • The severity of the violation.
  • The risk it poses to workers.
  • Whether it is a repeat violation.
  • The size of your payroll.
  • Your compliance history.

Repeat violations and violations that result in injury carry the highest penalties. Employers with a history of non-compliance face escalating consequences.

How to Avoid Orders and Penalties

The best way to avoid enforcement action is to be compliant before the inspector arrives. This means:

  • Having a current, adequate written safety program.
  • Conducting and documenting regular hazard assessments.
  • Training your workers and keeping records.
  • Conducting regular inspections and fixing hazards promptly.
  • Investigating incidents and implementing corrective actions.

If you do receive an order, comply by the deadline. If you need more time, request an extension before the deadline passes. Document everything.

What to Do If You Receive an Order

Read the order carefully. Understand what is being asked and by when. If anything is unclear, contact the issuing officer for clarification. Develop a plan to comply. Document your corrective actions. If you disagree with the order, you have the right to appeal, but you should still work toward compliance unless the order is stayed.

The Real Cost of Non-Compliance

The financial cost of orders and penalties is significant, but it is not the only cost. A stop-work order can shut down your operation for days or weeks. The reputational damage of a WorkSafeBC order can affect your ability to win contracts. And the human cost of the hazard that triggered the order is the most serious consequence of all.

The Order Hierarchy

Not all orders carry the same weight, and understanding the hierarchy helps you respond appropriately. At the most serious end, a stop-work order halts some or all activity until a hazard is addressed. These are reserved for situations where the officer believes work poses an imminent risk of serious injury. Complying quickly and professionally is the only sensible response; challenging it on site makes everything worse.

Below that are compliance orders with specific corrective actions and deadlines. These dominate routine inspections. Failing to meet a compliance deadline escalates the situation, often converting a manageable order into penalties and a much closer look at your overall program.

Penalties under the Workers Compensation Act can apply to employers, supervisors, and workers, depending on the violation. Amounts vary with the severity and history, and repeat findings compound quickly.

Your Options After Receiving an Order

You have the right to request a review of an order or a penalty. There are time limits, and the process is formal. If you believe an order is wrong, gather your documentation and act within the review window rather than ignoring it. Comply first where required, then pursue the review. Many employers do not realize that some orders can be stayed during review, while others cannot. Getting advice early protects your options.

How a Strong Program Prevents Orders

The employers who rarely receive orders share common traits. They identify hazards before someone reports them. They train consistently and keep records that prove it. They investigate incidents to root cause rather than stopping at the surface event. They treat orders, when they do receive them, as system signals rather than one-off bad luck. Every order contains information. The employers who read it save themselves the next one.

How Penalty Amounts Are Determined

Penalty amounts are not arbitrary. They reflect the nature of the violation, the degree of risk created, the size of the employer, and compliance history. A first, minor administrative lapse is treated differently from a repeat, high-risk violation. This is why a clean history is a genuine asset. Employers who fix problems promptly and document their efforts build a record that protects them when something eventually goes wrong.

The Supervisor and Worker Dimension

Responsibility under the Act is shared. Supervisors have specific duties, including ensuring workers follow safety requirements and that protective equipment is used. Workers have duties too, including taking reasonable care and following safety procedures. Penalties can apply to any of these parties. Smart employers train supervisors on their specific legal duties, because supervision failures are a recurring theme in penalty decisions. A supervisor who understands what the law expects is your first line of defence, not your exposure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an order and a penalty? An order requires corrective action by a deadline. A penalty is a financial sanction for a violation. They often arrive together, but an order can exist without a penalty and penalties can apply for conduct beyond a specific order.

Can workers receive penalties? Yes. Workers have duties under the Act, including taking reasonable care and following safety requirements. Penalties against workers are less common but entirely possible.

Do penalties affect my premiums? Indirectly, yes. Claims costs drive premium calculations, and penalty history affects how regulators and clients view your operation. A penalty is never just the dollar amount on the notice.

How long do I have to comply with an order? The order states its deadline. Treat it as immovable, complete the work early, and submit evidence before the date. If compliance is genuinely impossible in time, contact the officer before the deadline rather than after.

The Bottom Line

Orders and penalties are not random bad luck. They are the system's response to conditions that were visible before the officer arrived. Employers who run functioning programs rarely meet them, and employers who treat each order as a lesson rarely meet the same one twice. The practical takeaway is unglamorous: identify hazards, fix them, document the fix, and repeat. That cycle is both the compliance strategy and the protection strategy.
