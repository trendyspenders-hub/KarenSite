Fall Protection Planning for BC Construction Sites

Falls are the leading cause of serious injury in BC construction. Learn what fall protection plans must include and how to comply with WorkSafeBC requ

Falls from height are the leading cause of serious injury and death in BC construction. WorkSafeBC requires fall protection for any work above 3 metres (10 feet) or where a fall could cause injury, regardless of height. Despite this, fall protection violations are among the most common orders issued on BC construction sites.

This article explains what a fall protection plan must include, when it is required, and how to comply with WorkSafeBC requirements.

When Fall Protection Is Required

Under Part 11 of the OHS Regulation, fall protection is required when:

  • A worker could fall 3 metres or more.
  • A worker could fall less than 3 metres but could strike an object or surface below.
  • A worker is working above an open excavation or other hazard.

The type of fall protection required depends on the situation. The hierarchy of controls applies: elimination first, then engineering controls, then administrative controls, then personal protective equipment.

The Hierarchy of Fall Protection

1. Elimination

The best way to prevent falls is to eliminate the hazard entirely. Do the work at ground level. Use prefabrication to reduce work at height. Design out the need for workers to be elevated.

2. Engineering Controls

If you cannot eliminate the hazard, engineer it out. Guardrails are the most common engineering control. They provide collective protection that does not depend on individual worker behavior.

3. Administrative Controls

Warning lines, controlled access zones, and safety monitors are administrative controls. They restrict access to fall hazards but do not physically prevent falls. They are only acceptable in specific circumstances and at lower heights.

4. Personal Protective Equipment

Personal fall arrest systems (harnesses, lanyards, anchor points) are the last line of defense. They require individual worker action to use correctly. They are required when no higher-level control is feasible.

Fall Protection Plans

A written fall protection plan is required when workers are exposed to a fall of 7.5 metres (25 feet) or more, or when other fall protection methods are not practical. The plan must be specific to the site and the work.

A compliant fall protection plan includes:

  • A description of the work and the location.
  • The fall hazards identified.
  • The fall protection system to be used.
  • The anchor points and their load ratings.
  • The procedures for using the equipment.
  • The rescue plan.
  • The names of workers involved and their training.
  • The supervisor's name and signature.

Anchor Points

An anchor point is the connection point for a fall arrest system. It must be capable of supporting the forces generated by a fall. In BC, anchor points must support at least 22 kilonewtons (kN) or be designed by a professional engineer.

Common anchor points include structural steel, engineered anchors installed on roofs, and temporary anchors for scaffolding work. Never anchor to guardrails, pipes, or other non-structural elements.

Rescue Plans

A fall arrest system stops the fall. It does not get the worker down. A worker suspended in a harness can develop suspension trauma within minutes. Blood pools in the legs, reducing circulation to the brain and vital organs.

Your fall protection plan must include a rescue plan. This means having the equipment and trained personnel to rescue a suspended worker quickly. The plan must be practiced before it is needed.

Training Requirements

All workers who use fall protection equipment must be trained on its proper use, inspection, and limitations. Training must be documented. In BC, fall protection training is available through several providers and is typically a one-day course.

Workers must also be trained on the specific fall hazards of their workplace and the procedures in the fall protection plan.

Common Fall Protection Mistakes

The most common mistakes we see on BC construction sites are:

  • Harnesses worn but not connected to an anchor point.
  • Anchors that are not rated or are attached to non-structural elements.
  • Lanyards that are too long, allowing a fall to exceed the safe distance.
  • No rescue plan in place.
  • Fall protection plans that are generic and not site-specific.
  • Workers using equipment they have not been trained on.

All of these mistakes can be fatal.

Inspection and Maintenance

Fall protection equipment must be inspected before each use. Harnesses, lanyards, and connectors must be checked for wear, damage, and corrosion. Equipment that has been subjected to a fall must be removed from service immediately.

Equipment must be maintained according to the manufacturer's instructions. Storage matters too. Equipment stored in direct sunlight, extreme temperatures, or near chemicals degrades faster.

When Fall Protection Is Required

In British Columbia, fall protection is required when a worker faces a fall hazard of three metres or more into a lower level, or where the fall could result in injury regardless of height. That second condition surprises people. A two-metre fall onto rebar, equipment, or a concrete edge can be fatal, so the three-metre rule is a floor, not a ceiling.

Construction adds complexity because fall hazards change as the project progresses. The exposure during framing differs from during roofing, which differs from during finishing. A fall protection plan that works in week two may be inadequate by week six. This is why the plan must be dynamic, reviewed at each phase change, not filed at project start.

Choosing the Right System

The hierarchy matters. Elimination and guardrails come before personal fall arrest systems wherever feasible. Guardrails protect everyone without relying on individual behaviour. Nets serve intermediate situations. Personal systems, harnesses, lanyards, and anchors, are the last line because they depend on correct selection, fitting, connection, and rescue.

When personal systems are used, the details decide whether they work:

  • Anchages must be rated and positioned to limit free fall and swing fall.
  • Lanyard length must keep arresting forces and clearance within limits.
  • Harnesses must fit properly and be inspected before each use.
  • A rescue plan must exist, because suspension after an arrest is a medical emergency in minutes.

The Rescue Problem

Suspended workers can lose consciousness within minutes due to suspension trauma. Many construction sites have harnesses and lanyards but no realistic rescue capability. A written rescue plan, equipment on site, and workers trained to execute it are not optional extras. Regulators increasingly treat rescue planning as a core element of any fall protection program, and they are right to do so.

Documentation the Plan Must Include

A fall protection plan is a written document, and it must address specific content: the fall hazards at the site, the systems selected for each hazard, anchor specifications and locations, rescue procedures, and training requirements for the workers involved. Generic plans copied between projects fail here, because sites differ. A plan for a wood-frame residential project differs from one for steel erection or commercial roofing.

Supervisors should carry the plan or know it thoroughly, since they answer for it during inspections. Workers covered by the plan must be trained on its content before exposure, with records kept. The plan is also a living document: when the phase changes, the plan changes, and the revision is documented like any other controlled document.

Spot Checks That Keep Compliance Real

Program documents drift from practice without active supervision. Effective site leads run regular spot checks: harness fit and condition, correct connection to anchors, guardrail integrity after material deliveries, and lanyard routing that avoids sharp edges. Each check takes minutes. Together they keep the plan functioning between formal inspections, and they signal to the crew that fall protection is a daily expectation, not an audit-day performance.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what height is fall protection required? In BC, the general trigger is a fall of three metres or more, but protection is required at any height where injury could result. Treat three metres as a minimum, not a threshold that excuses lower exposures.

Who writes the fall protection plan? A competent person prepares it for the specific site, and it must be available to workers and inspectors. Many employers use consultants for the template and site supervisors for site-specific adaptation.

Is a harness enough? No. A harness is one component of a system that includes a rated anchor, connecting hardware, adequate clearance, and a rescue plan. Any missing component can turn an arrested fall into a fatality.

Do residential projects need the same rigor? The requirements apply by hazard, not project size. Falls from residential heights injure workers regularly, and inspectors apply the same expectations on houses as on commercial towers.

The Bottom Line

Fall protection fails in the details, and the details are manageable. Plans matched to the site, systems selected by hierarchy, anchors verified, workers trained, and rescue arranged before the first exposure. Construction will always involve heights. What is optional is whether the exposure is controlled. Employers who treat fall protection as a planning discipline rather than an equipment purchase see the difference in their incident record and their premiums, year after year.
