Plain Language

The Terms, Decoded

Four phrases you will meet on this site and in every bid or inspection, in one line each.

OHS

Occupational Health and Safety — the body of law and regulation that governs workplace safety. In BC that is primarily the WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation; other provinces have their own equivalents.

COR

Certificate of Recognition — a voluntary audit program that certifies a company's safety program meets a national standard. Many prime contractors and owners require it (or its audit score) before awarding work.

Hazard assessment

A structured look at what on your worksite can hurt people — equipment, tasks, materials, environment — scored by risk so the most dangerous things get controlled first.

Safe work procedures

Short, step-by-step instructions for a specific higher-risk task, written for the worker doing it — not a policy paragraph, a recipe.

Safety Plan Coordination, Explained

What a Safety Plan Coordinator Actually Does for Your Business

In short: a safety plan coordinator designs, writes, and implements the workplace safety program your business is legally required to have – then stays involved until it passes inspection. Instead of downloading a generic template and hoping for the best, you get a documented program built around your actual facility, workforce, and regulators.

Most business owners know they need a safety program. Far fewer know what "good" looks like – until an inspector, an insurance auditor, or an injured worker's lawyer tells them. That's the gap a safety plan coordinator closes.

Why Generic Safety Plan Templates Keep Failing Businesses

Search for a workplace safety plan online and you'll find thousands of templates. They fail for one simple reason: regulators don't inspect your paperwork against a template – they inspect it against your floor. A plan that references equipment you don't own, procedures your team has never seen, and hazards that don't exist at your site is worse than no plan at all, because it proves you documented a program you never implemented.

Every engagement at Alcantara Safety Solutions starts with an on-site assessment. We walk your facility, interview your workers, and review your incident history before a single page is written. The result is a plan that describes your operation – which is exactly what WorkSafeBC officers expect to see.

The Real Cost of Getting Safety Compliance Wrong

WorkSafeBC administrative penalties for high-risk violations can run into the hundreds of thousands of dollars. But fines are rarely the biggest number. But fines are rarely the biggest number. A single lost-time injury typically costs employers tens of thousands of dollars in direct costs and several times that in indirect costs: production delays, retraining, equipment damage, morale, and insurance premium increases that follow you for years.

Compare that to the cost of a professionally built safety program. Most of our projects range from $1,200 to $7,500 depending on scope – less than one serious fine, and a fraction of one lost-time injury. For most businesses, a custom safety plan is one of the highest-return investments available.

How to Choose Between Our Three Core Services

Choose Safety Plan Creation if you're starting from scratch – or starting over

If your business has no written program, or your current one is a template binder nobody has opened in years, a custom safety plan is the right foundation. You get the full program: hazard assessments, safe work procedures, training matrices, emergency protocols, and a documentation system organized for inspection.

Choose Compliance Consulting if you have a program but doubt it

If you have documentation but aren't confident it would survive an inspection – or you've received a citation, an order, or an audit failure – a compliance gap review maps your program against every applicable regulation and gives you a prioritized fix list with realistic deadlines.

Choose a Risk Assessment if you need to know where the danger is

If injuries, near-misses, or insurance costs are trending the wrong way, a workplace risk assessment identifies and scores every hazard in your operation, then ranks them so you know exactly what to fix first. Many clients start here, then roll the findings into a full safety plan.

Good Fit

Who This Suits

An honest picture of the businesses this work is built for — and a few it is not.

Business sizes we support well

Employers with roughly 1–200 workers. Below about 10 workers you likely need a lean core program rather than a full binder — we scope accordingly. Above roughly 200 workers, most organizations are better served by a dedicated in-house safety manager, and we will say so.

Operating situations

Operations with physical, regulated work: construction sites, fabrication and manufacturing floors, warehouses and yards, civil and infrastructure projects, and facilities such as care homes. Home-based or office-only businesses rarely need this level of documentation.

Project types

New programs written from scratch, overhauls of outdated manuals, pre-bid safety packages, COR preparation, and documentation rebuilt after an inspection, audit, or order. One-off paperwork reviews are fine; partial edits of a stranger's template rarely are.

One site or many

Single-site programs are scoped to that location. For contractors with changing sites or several crews, the core program stays constant while site-specific addenda — tailboard talks, site hazard assessments, per-project procedures — are issued per site or per crew, so the paperwork follows the work instead of the other way around.

Not a fit (for now)

Large enterprise multi-provincial programs needing a permanent embedded manager, union-specific CBAs, and substance-testing policy design each call for specialist providers. If that is you, we will say so in the first call and, where we can, point you toward a better-suited route.

Documents your crew can actually use

Approved deliverables are delivered in PDF for phones, tablets, and printing on site, and in editable formats (Word) so your team can maintain them. Everything is written in plain language sized for a tailgate meeting — not binder filler.
